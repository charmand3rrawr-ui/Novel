# Chapter 2 — Expiring

He did not get up for a long time, and the reason he did not get up was not shock.

It was that getting up would require a decision about which of the impossible things to accept first, and Adam had a professional prejudice against making decisions in the wrong order.

So he lay in the wet grass with his cheek against the cold of it and did what he would have done with any file that arrived on his desk in a state like this: he stopped trying to understand it and started trying to *bound* it.

Item. He was breathing. He established this by paying attention to it, and then by holding his breath for a count of nine to check that the breathing was his and not something happening to him. It was his. It hurt slightly at the top of the inhale, on the right side, low.

Item. The pain on the right side, low, was in the correct location.

He got his hand down and under his shirt — a shirt, he noted, that was not his shirt; it was rough and undyed and laced rather than buttoned — and put two fingers against the skin below his ribs.

There was a scar there. Not a wound. A scar, hard and raised and perhaps a decade old by the feel of it, and when he pressed it there was a dull ache that went somewhere internal and then stopped.

He took his hand out and looked at his fingers, and they were clean, and they were also not his fingers. They were the right shape. The nails were wrong — square where his were oval — and there was a callus across the top of the palm that he had never had in his life, a working callus, the kind you get from a tool you hold the same way every day for years.

He lay there and turned the hand over twice in the moonlight.

*So: not a resuscitation. Not a hospital. Not a hallucination in the eleven minutes, because eleven minutes does not produce a decade-old scar.*

He was aware that there was an obvious conclusion, and that the obvious conclusion was insane, and he set it to one side unexamined. Not because he was frightened of it. Because it was not yet load-bearing. He could function without settling it, and the things he could not function without were more urgent.

Item. The sky.

He made himself look at it properly, which took more than he expected.

Two moons. One large and low and the colour of a bad tooth; one small, much higher, very white, and moving — actually visibly moving, if he held his eye on a star and waited. The stars themselves were wrong in a way he could not specify, because he had never been the sort of man who knew the constellations, which struck him now as a serious and irreparable gap in his preparation.

There was also a band across the sky, faint, about a third of the way up. Not the Milky Way. Too regular. It had structure.

He looked at it for perhaps a minute and then filed it, deliberately, under *not urgent*, and felt something unpleasant go through him at the phrase.

Item. The field.

Wet grass, shin-high, going away in every direction into dark. Ground sloping down and to his left. A line of trees on the ridge behind him, black against the sky. No walls, no fences that he could see.

And — he sat up for this, slowly, and the world did a slow revolution and settled — a glow on the horizon to the left, low and orange and steady.

Not a fire. Fires flicker. This did not flicker. This was the light of somewhere with more than one light in it, and it was perhaps four or five miles off, and it was the single most valuable object in his field of view.

Item, finally, the thing he had been not looking at.

The words were still there.

They sat in the middle of his vision, and they did not move when his eyes moved, and they were not glowing, exactly; they were simply *legible*, in the way that a thing you have just read is legible for a moment after you look away.

**ASSESSMENT PENDING.**
**SUBJECT: UNREGISTERED.**
**LOCAL RESIDUE DETECTED — 1 SOURCE, EXPIRING.**

Adam Rooke looked at the third line for some time.

Then he said, out loud, in a voice that came out of a throat that was not the throat he had used for thirty-eight years:

"Expiring at what rate?"

The words did not answer. He had not really expected them to. But something did happen: the third line brightened very slightly, and a thread — that was the only word for it, a thin line of the faintest possible colour — appeared in the air in front of him and lay along the ground, down the slope, to the left, away into the dark.

He looked at it for a while.

Then he got up, which was harder than it should have been, and followed it.

---

The body he was walking in was thirty years old at the outside and had never done anything difficult.

He established this over the first quarter-mile with the same grim thoroughness he had brought to the sky. The legs worked. The wind was poor — he was blowing within four hundred yards, on a shallow slope, which was worse than he would have been at thirty-eight after a year of not going to the gym. There was no strength anywhere in the arms. The callus on the hand was a liar; whatever this body had done for a living, it had done sitting down.

He was, in short, in the worst physical condition of any adult male he had ever underwritten.

He noted this without distress. It was data. Distress was a thing that would arrive later, and he had a fairly clear sense that when it did arrive it would be enormous and would take a long time, and that this was an argument for getting as much done as possible first.

The thread led him down the slope and along the bottom of the field to a gap in a hedge and through it into the next field, and the grass here was shorter and cropped, and there was a smell he did not immediately place.

Then he did place it, because he had smelled it in the kitchen four hours or eleven years ago, depending on how you counted.

Blood.

He stopped at the gap in the hedge.

It occurred to him — very clearly, standing there in a dead man's shirt under two moons — that he had a choice available to him, and that the choice was real, and that he was almost certainly going to make it incorrectly.

He went through the hedge.

---

The animal was lying on its side about thirty feet in, and it was very large.

He had no name for it. It was roughly the mass of a carthorse and roughly the shape of one, but the head was too heavy and too low-slung, and the legs were wrong — too many joints, or joints in the wrong places — and it had a ridge of coarse grey plating down the spine that caught the moonlight like wet slate.

There was a crossbow bolt in it, high on the shoulder, and it had bled a great deal.

It was still alive. Its flank went up and down, shallow and fast, and there was a sound coming out of it at each exhale — not a bellow, not a cry, just a flat mechanical sound like air through a broken reed.

Adam stood ten feet away and did not go closer.

The thread ran from his chest to the animal's chest, and now that he was near, the words had changed.

**SOURCE: DRAFT-BEAST (DOMESTICATED) — TERMINAL**
**ESTIMATED TIME TO RESIDUE: 6–11 MINUTES**
**PROJECTED YIELD: 3–4**
**ABSORB? Y/N**

He read it three times.

Then he said, "Three to four *what*," and the words did not change, and he thought: *of course. Of course no units. Nobody ever gives you the units. They give you the figure and let you assume, and the assumption is where they get you.*

He looked at the animal.

It was, he thought, someone's. It had a harness on — a broad leather collar, scarred and mended, with a brass ring at the throat — and somebody had spent money on that harness, and had probably spent a long time on the animal, and was probably at this moment somewhere in the dark with a lantern, looking.

Somebody had shot it. Or somebody had shot at something else and hit it. Or it had been poached and the poachers had been disturbed. There were a number of scenarios and he could construct the probabilities on none of them, because he had been in this world for something under forty minutes and had exactly zero priors.

He found he had walked closer without deciding to.

The eye was the bad part. It was enormous, and brown, and there was nothing behind it that he could make any judgement about at all, and it was not looking at him. That was somehow much worse than if it had been. It was looking past him at the hedge, and it was doing the thing that dying animals do, which is to keep on attending to the world in the ordinary way right up until they stop.

He crouched down in the wet grass, about four feet from its head, with his forearms on his knees, and stayed there.

**ESTIMATED TIME TO RESIDUE: 4–8 MINUTES**

"Yes," he said. "I can see that."

He did not look away from the eye.

He was aware — with the clarity that had been the great curse of his life — that he was performing something. That nobody was watching, that the animal did not know he was there in any sense that mattered, that crouching here in the cold was not a kindness to anything, and that he was doing it so that later he would be able to say he had.

He was aware, further, that being aware of this did not stop him wanting to do it.

"It isn't," he said, "going to be quick."

The flank went up. Went down. The broken-reed sound.

"I don't know what I am now." He turned his hands over and looked at them, the square nails, the liar's callus. "I've worked out roughly what's happened to me and I've decided not to look at it directly yet. I'm going to have to at some point. I'd rather do it indoors."

Up. Down.

"I'm going to take whatever it is you've got," he said. "I want to be clear about that, because it's the sort of thing a person should be clear about. I'm not sitting here instead of taking it. I'm sitting here *and* taking it. Those are very different things and I'd like at least one of us to know which is happening."

**ESTIMATED TIME TO RESIDUE: 2–4 MINUTES**

The eye did not move.

Adam Rooke, who had never in his life been able to leave a thing crooked, reached out and put his hand flat on the great slab of the animal's cheek, where the hair was short and coarse and shockingly warm, and held it there.

"It's all right," he said, which was a lie, and which was the first lie he had told in this world. "It's all right. You're all right."

The flank went up.

It did not go down.

---

The residue came up out of it the way steam comes off a road.

He would spend a long time, later, trying to improve on that description, and he never would, because it was exactly right. It did not glow. It did not swirl. It rose off the whole length of the body in a thin, even, unhurried sheet, entirely colourless, visible only because it bent the moonlight very slightly as it went, the way hot air over tarmac bends a horizon.

It rose about two feet and then it began to thin out, and he understood without being told that when it had thinned out it would be gone, and that this was what *expiring* meant, and that it had nothing to do with the animal's death at all. The animal had died. This was something else, leaving afterwards, at its own rate, whether anyone was there or not.

**ABSORB? Y/N**

"Yes," said Adam.

It came to him.

It did not rush. It leaned — that was the sensation, the whole sheet of it leaning off the body and across the four feet of grass between them, unhurried, the way smoke leans towards an open door — and it went into his chest and his forearms and the flats of his hands, and it was not warm and it was not cold.

It was *full*.

That was the only word. For about two seconds Adam Rooke was full, in the way a glass is full, and the sensation had no location and no temperature and no edge, and it was without any question the single most pleasurable thing that had ever happened to him in either life.

Then it stopped, and he was kneeling in a wet field beside a dead animal, breathing hard.

**ABSORBED.**
**STRENGTH +2**
**CONSTITUTION +1**

**TOTAL RESIDUE ASSIMILATED: 3**

He stayed where he was for a while.

The strength was real. That was the thing that frightened him, once he had got his breath, and it frightened him considerably more than the words had. He could *feel* it — not as power, nothing so cinematic, but as a change in the baseline. His forearms felt as they had felt at twenty-four, in the six months he had gone to a gym with a girlfriend who had left him in the November. His grip, when he made a fist, closed differently.

Three units of residue. Two into strength, one into constitution, and nobody had asked him how he wanted them allocated.

*Which means it either allocates by the shape of the source, or by need, or at random.* He turned the possibilities over. *A draft animal. Strength and constitution. It allocated by the shape of the source.*

He looked at the dead thing in the grass. At the harness with the brass ring. At the crossbow bolt.

And he thought, before he could stop himself, in the flat inward voice he used for numbers:

*It was projecting three to four. It gave three. It was down at the bottom of the range — because I waited.*

He heard the thought arrive. He did not push it away.

*Six to eleven minutes it said, at the start. And the projection dropped as it went. So the yield decays with the dying. Which means the maximum is at the moment of death, or as near to it as you can arrange, and every minute you spend crouching in the grass telling an animal it's all right costs you—*

He stopped.

He got up off his knees, and he was unsteady, and he stood in the dark field with the body at his feet and made himself finish the sentence, because a man who does not finish the sentence is simply a man who intends to think it again later with less supervision.

*—costs you roughly a quarter of a unit a minute. And you could have put your hand on the bolt and pushed.*

The field was very quiet. Somewhere back up the slope, an animal that was not this one made a noise and stopped.

"No," said Adam, out loud.

And then, because he had never in his life been able to leave a thing crooked, he added, honestly:

"Not yet."

---

Before he left the field he did one more thing, and he was careful, afterwards, not to lie to himself about why.

He put both hands under the edge of the animal's shoulder and tried to lift it.

It did not move — it was six or seven hundred kilos of dead weight and he was not a crane — but the *attempt* told him what he wanted. There was a moment at the top of the effort, before his back gave out, where he felt the load and the load was legible. He could tell, the way you can tell the weight of a suitcase before you decide whether to carry it, that this was a thing he could have shifted at perhaps four times his current strength.

Four times. From three units.

He let go and stood back and wiped his hands down his borrowed shirt.

*So the scale is not linear, or three units is not a small number, or both.* He had no way to distinguish between those and he knew it. One data point and no baseline was worse than useless; it was the kind of thing that made people confident. *I need a second measurement and a third before I let myself believe any of this.*

But underneath that, in the place where the actuarial voice did not reach, something else had already happened and was not going to un-happen.

For two seconds in a wet field he had been *full*.

He had been thirty-eight years old and then dead and then thirty and illiterate and hunted by nothing at all, and for two seconds none of that had been the most important fact about him.

He stood in the dark and made himself look at that squarely, the way he had made himself look at the repricing, because it was the more dangerous of the two by an enormous margin.

"Noted," he said.

Then he took the harness off it.

It took him eleven minutes and the buckles were stiff and he cut the side of his hand on the brass ring, and he did it because the harness was worth money and he had no money, and because a man walking out of a field carrying tack looks like a man who has lost an animal, whereas a man walking out of a field carrying nothing looks like whatever the person who finds him decides he looks like.

He was already, he noticed, thinking in terms of how he would look to people who had not yet seen him.

The words were gone from his vision. He had tried, experimentally, to make them come back — thinking *assessment*, thinking *status*, thinking, in a moment of genuine embarrassment, *menu* — and nothing had happened.

Then, walking, with his mind entirely on the orange glow on the horizon, they appeared for a second unbidden:

**ASSESSMENT PENDING.**
**SUBJECT: UNREGISTERED.**

And were gone again.

He filed that. *Not on request. On trigger. Find the trigger.*

He had been walking for about fifty minutes, and had just found a road — rutted, unmetalled, going roughly the right way — when the first one passed him.

He heard it a long time before he saw it. A low hum, steady, without any of the reciprocation of an engine, rising in pitch as it came. He got off the road because getting off the road seemed obviously correct, and stood in the ditch with the harness in his arms like a man holding a coat.

It came round the bend doing perhaps forty miles an hour and it did not touch the ground.

It was a flatbed — that was the honest description, a flatbed truck about eighteen feet long, loaded with crates under a lashed tarpaulin — and it rode about eight inches above the ruts on nothing at all, and where the wheels should have been there were four housings, squat and finned, each with a slow blue light turning over inside it. There was a cab at the front with a man in it. There were two lamps on the front of the cab, and they were electric, or something so close to electric that Adam's brain accepted them without comment.

There was also, running the length of the flatbed under the tarpaulin lashings, a line of characters in the same script as the milestone, painted in white, with what was unmistakably a company logo at the end of it.

It went past him in about four seconds, and the wash of it moved the grass, and it was gone round the next bend, and the hum dropped away down the scale.

Adam stood in the ditch for some time.

Then he climbed back onto the road and sat down on the bank, because his legs had made the decision independently.

He had, he realised, been carrying an assumption. He had been carrying it since the field, and it had been shaping everything he had thought since — every plan, every estimate of his own position — and he had never once taken it out and looked at it, because it had been too obvious to seem like an assumption at all.

He had assumed *backwards*.

Two moons, wet grass, a draft animal in a leather harness, a dirt road, a man in a rough laced shirt. It had all pointed one way and he had followed it without noticing he was following anything. Somewhere in the back of his head he had been constructing a world of ox-carts and lantern-light and men with swords, and he had been quietly, enormously comforted by it, because a world like that is a world in which a clever man from a later century arrives holding a stack of free advantages.

He had been, without ever putting it into words, planning to be the cleverest man on the planet.

"Right," he said aloud, to the empty road. "That's gone, then."

He made himself sit with it, because this was the part that mattered and he knew it. It was not a setback. It was a *repricing*, and he had spent sixteen years watching people take repricings badly — watching them argue with the number, or pay the old premium out of spite, or simply put the letter in a drawer.

The correct response to a repricing is to say the new number out loud.

"I am not ahead of them," said Adam. "I am not even level. I am a thirty-year-old illiterate with no trade, on a road, at night, in a world that has haulage."

It was worse than that, and he made himself go on until he had all of it.

Because a world with haulage has records. A world with records has registration, and the words in his vision had used that exact term about him, twice, and had used it the way a form uses it. *SUBJECT: UNREGISTERED.* Not *unknown*. Not *foreign*. Unregistered was a status in a system that had other statuses, and he had spent his entire adult life working inside institutions and he knew exactly what unregistered meant everywhere he had ever seen it, which was: *no claim, no recourse, no premium paid, nobody comes when you call.*

He had spent eleven hours at nine years old learning what that felt like from the inside, and here it was again, printed in his own eye.

The second one came past twenty minutes later going the other way, and he did not bother getting into the ditch, and the driver did not look at him.

The third was not a truck.

He heard nothing at all. He was walking with his head down, doing the sums on his own stride length to estimate the distance remaining, and there was a pressure at the back of his neck like the pressure before a storm, and he turned round.

A woman was going up the road behind him, walking, about sixty feet back, and she was going very fast.

That was all. She was not running. Her arms were at her sides and her gait was the unhurried gait of a person crossing a room, and she was moving up that rutted road at something close to the speed of the flatbed, and the dust was not rising behind her.

She glanced at him as she came level. It was not a hostile look and it was not a friendly one; it was the look you give a bollard, to confirm it is a bollard. She had a canvas satchel across her body and a coat with a high collar and she was perhaps fifty.

She was past him and thirty feet gone before he had finished turning his head, and the pressure at the back of his neck went with her, and he understood — with the complete, sick clarity of a man watching a figure move in the wrong column — that whatever she was doing had nothing to do with machinery.

He stood in the road and watched her out of sight.

Then he laughed. It surprised him. It came out once, flat and unamused, and it startled a bird somewhere in the hedge.

Because the sums were now genuinely interesting.

A world with haulage *and* that. Two ladders, not one. And he had climbed exactly three units of one of them, off a dying animal, in a field, and it had felt like the best thing that had ever happened to him.

"Three," he said. "Three units. And she walks at forty miles an hour."

He started walking again, and he was smiling slightly, and if anyone had been there to see it they would not have enjoyed it.

Because there was one more thing in the sums and he had got to it now, and it was the only genuinely good news he had received since the kitchen floor.

That woman had gone past him at forty miles an hour without breaking stride, and she had looked at him, and she had seen a bollard.

*Nobody*, thought Adam Rooke, *is going to look at me twice for a very long time.*

*Which is the cheapest thing I will ever own.*

---

The walk to the lights took two and a half hours in total and by the end of it his legs had stopped being a source of information and become simply a problem. Twice he had to sit down. The second time he sat on a milestone at the edge of the road, and he could not read what was cut into it, and he sat in the dark holding a stranger's harness and looking at letters he could not read, and *that* was the thing that finally got through.

Not the moons. Not the animal. Not the words.

The letters.

He had read professionally for sixteen years. He had been, in the only sense that had ever mattered to him, a man who read things other people could not read and told them what the things said. And he sat on a cold stone in the dark and looked at six characters cut into it and they meant absolutely nothing, and he put his head down on his forearms and stayed like that for some time.

When he lifted his head the small white moon had moved appreciably and his face was wet and he had a headache behind the eyes.

He got up. He picked up the harness.

"All right," he said, to nobody, in a voice that was almost steady. "All right. Assumptions."

He started walking.

"One. I'm dead, and this is somewhere else, and it's a real somewhere else with weather and livestock and roads, so it has rules, and rules can be learned." He counted it off on his fingers as he walked, which is a thing he had done since he was a child and had been mocked for at least twice. "Two. I have something nobody's mentioned to me, which means either nobody has it or nobody admits to having it, and both of those are dangerous but they're dangerous in opposite directions, so until I know which, I say nothing to anybody about anything."

The lights were resolving now. Not a town. A cluster — a long low building with lit windows, a wall, outbuildings, and a road going up to it with a gate.

"Three." He stopped on the road with the harness over his shoulder and looked at the lit windows, and worked out, from the pattern of them, that whatever it was, it was awake at four in the morning, and that there were only a few kinds of building that are awake at four in the morning. "Three. I'm thirty years old, I can't read, I can't fight, I don't know what money looks like, and I am, on the evidence, extremely easy to kill."

He shifted the harness.

"So the first thing is not revenge," he said, reasonably, to the empty road. "The first thing is a roof and a meal and a word for where I am. Revenge is a luxury good. You buy it late."

He was aware, even as he said it — and this was the part he would think about, much later, on a different world, with a number in his head that had eleven digits in it — that he had just said the word *revenge* out loud for the first time, on the road, at four in the morning, without ever having decided to.

He had not planned it. It had simply been in the sentence, waiting, the way it had been in him since the kitchen floor.

Adam Rooke walked up the road towards the lit windows.

Above the gate, cut into a stone lintel in letters he could not read, was the name of the place. He stopped under it and looked up, and the two lines of it meant nothing to him, and he stood there a moment anyway with the harness biting into his shoulder, because it seemed to him that a man ought to at least look at the name of a thing before he walked into it.

Then he went in, because it was cold, and because there was nowhere else.

He would learn to read the lintel within the month. He would think about it, on and off, for the rest of his life — not the words, which were ordinary, but the fact that he had stood under them illiterate and hungry and had gone in anyway, and that they had let him.
