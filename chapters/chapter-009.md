# Chapter 9 — Tempering

Varn came in at ten past four with her sleeves already rolled, and Petren was on the running board of the car with his arms round the post, blue about the mouth, having stood on the Quarry Road for two hours and forty minutes in a wind off the ridge.

Adam took him off the board and carried him — carried him, which he could not have done six weeks earlier — into the intake room and put him on the bench and wrapped him in two blankets and put his own hands round the boy's feet, and Petren said, "I flagged her," with enormous satisfaction, and then could not say anything else for a while.

"You flagged her," said Adam. "She'd have gone past. She'd have gone straight past to Second and we'd have had nothing until the morning."

"I know."

"Petren. Look at me. She'd have gone past."

"I *know*," said the boy, and fell asleep sitting up.

Adam sat with his hands round a nine-year-old's feet for ninety seconds, which was all he had, and then went back out into the yard.

---

Varn worked for thirty-one hours.

She stopped four times, for eleven minutes each, and she ate standing up and she did not sit down once, and by the eleventh hour her apprentices had gone to pieces — one of them properly, the young man, who went out into the yard and was sick and then wept and then came back and was, from that point on, genuinely useful, which Adam noted and filed as something he did not understand about people.

She pulled Adam into the theatre at hour six and never let him out again.

"You hold. You count. You tell me what you see." She did not look up. "You do not touch anything I have not put your hands on. If you think I am wrong you say so immediately and you say it once, and if I say no you do what I said. Yes?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

"I hold, I count, I tell you what I see. I say it once."

"Good." She was already cutting. "Left hand on the retractor. There. That is the whole of your job for the next four hours and it is the most important job in this room."

---

He learned more medicine in thirty-one hours than in the preceding six weeks, and almost none of it was technique.

What he learned was *sequence*. Varn's hands did nothing he could have imitated, but the order she did things in had a logic, and the logic was recoverable, and by hour nine he could predict her next instrument about a third of the time and by hour nineteen about two thirds, and at some point in the twenties she stopped saying what she wanted and simply held her hand out.

He also learned the thing she had been unable to teach her apprentices in four years, which was when to stop.

He watched her stop three times. Each time it was the same movement — the back of the left hand laid flat against the sternum, two or three seconds, eyes somewhere else — and each time it was followed by a short sentence and a closing.

The third time, Adam said, "Why."

"Because he's gone and he doesn't know it yet."

"How do you know?"

"I can feel what's moving." She was already washing. "It's not mystical. It's the same as you and your columns. You can see the shape of a page. I can feel the shape of a man's essence and when it starts to let go of the edges, it lets go from the edges inward, and once it's past the wrists there's nothing at the middle to save."

She looked up at him.

"You don't have it," she said. It was not a question and it was not unkind. "Four Thursdays I've watched you and you read everything with your eyes. Whatever you are, you're not a sensitive."

*No,* thought Adam, holding a retractor in a room with three dead men waiting outside the door. *I'm not a sensitive. I'm a creditor, and I can see exactly when the account closes, and I am the only person on this planet who can.*

"No," he said. "I'm not."

He counted. Of course he counted.

Four feet. He was four feet away, holding a lamp, and he could see the boy's face and the boy's face was turned towards the lamp because light is what you look at.

*Eight,* thought Adam. *Eight, and he is fourteen, and there is no arrangement of events in which he gets up off that cot, and I know that, and Varn knows it, and Wren knows it, and in thirty-one minutes it goes into the rafters and nobody has it.*

And then, arriving underneath it with the smooth inevitability of a thing that has been waiting:

*And if I take it, precisely nothing about his last half hour is different.*

That was the argument. He heard it all the way through, at four feet, holding a lamp, and it was *good*. It was better than any argument he had constructed for himself in six weeks. Nothing in it was false. The boy would not know. The boy could not be helped. The residue was going to go whether Adam Rooke stood here or in the yard or on the road to the Quarry, and the only variable in the entire system was whether it went to waste.

He held the lamp steady for Tolen for another nineteen minutes.

The sheet came up off him in the lamplight, unhurried, bending the light very slightly the way hot air over tarmac bends a horizon.

Adam sat holding a dead boy's hand and did not move, and the pain came up in his sternum and the insides of his forearms and stayed for as long as the residue took to thin, which was about two minutes, and it was worse than the third time and not as bad as the seventh.

"No," he said.

And it went.

And afterwards, walking back to the theatre, he understood that he had done something that was not on any of his ledgers: he had said it out loud, to nobody, in an empty part of a ward at nine in the evening, because he had needed to *hear* it.

*Which means I no longer entirely trust myself to have meant it silently.*

---

And so on, through the night, eleven times.

He said no eleven times.

He did not say it out loud. There was no moment of struggle, no hand hovering, nothing a camera would have recorded; there was a man holding a retractor in a tack room while a number hung in his vision, and the number thinned and went, and the man did not move.

But somewhere around the seventh one, it started to hurt.

Not metaphorically. It was a physical sensation and it had a location, and the location was the flat of his sternum and the insides of his forearms — exactly where the residue had gone in, in a wet field, six weeks earlier.

It came up when the words came up and it went when the residue thinned, and each time it was worse.

By the eleventh it was bad enough that he had to breathe through it, and Varn said, without looking up, "Are you hurt?"

"No."

"You went white."

"I'm tired."

"Everyone here is tired." She held her hand out for the clamp. "You went white four times in six hours, at intervals, and you're not bleeding. I'll ask you again when we're through and you'll give me a better answer."

---

At hour twenty-four he stopped being tired in any way he recognised.

He had never worked like this. He had done eighty-hour weeks in his first life, in the years when the firm was pitching, and he understood now that those had been eighty hours of *sitting down being anxious*, and that he had been proud of them, and the pride embarrassed him.

This was different. This was thirty-one hours of a body at load, and somewhere in the second night his body stopped negotiating and simply did it.

The pain in the sternum receded. The shaking in his hands went. His vision narrowed to the width of the table and stayed there, and he found that instructions took less time to travel, and that Varn had stopped saying his name before speaking to him.

Somewhere in hour twenty-six, he noticed he was warm.

It was the first thing he had noticed about himself in a day. He had been cold since the field — properly cold, the low grinding cold of a body running under its own capacity, which he had assumed was this world or this body or grief.

He was not cold. He was warm from the middle outward, and the warmth was in the marrow of his forearms and along the front of his shins, and it was not pleasant.

"Varn."

"Don't talk."

"Varn, I need to be replaced on this in about four minutes."

The surgeon's head came up for the first time in an hour and a half.

"Say that again."

"I don't know what's happening to me. I think something is." He kept his hands exactly where they were. "It might be nothing and I might be wrong, and if I'm wrong I'll have cost you four minutes. If I'm right and I'm still holding this when it happens, I don't know what my hands will do."

Ilse Varn looked at him across a body for about two seconds.

Then she said, "Tolen. Take his place. Rooke, step back on my count — three, two—"

---

He did not get four minutes. He got about fifty seconds.

He had stepped back from the table and was two paces towards the door when it took him, and the way he described it afterwards — the only way he ever found — was that somebody put a hand inside his chest and *opened* it.

There was no pain. That was the obscenity of it. There was heat, enormous and total, running out from the sternum along the arms and down into the legs, and every place it reached came apart and went back together slightly differently, and it was the single most pleasurable thing that had ever happened to him, and it lasted eleven seconds, and it was four times what the field had been.

Afterwards he tried to work out what had actually happened in those eleven seconds, and the closest he ever came was this.

Something had been *tight*.

He had not known it. That was the thing. For six weeks — for thirty-eight years, possibly, though that was the kind of thought he distrusted — there had been a tightness through the middle of him that was so total and so constant that it had not registered as a sensation at all, any more than a man registers the pressure of the air.

And the heat went through it and it *let go*.

The bones of his forearms went first. He felt them — actually felt them, from the inside, which is not a thing a body is built to report — go porous and then dense again, the whole architecture of the marrow rearranging in about two seconds, and the sensation of it was like being stretched after sleeping badly, multiplied by some factor he could not name.

Then the shins. Then the ribs, one at a time, front to back, in a sequence so orderly it felt designed.

Then his lungs took a breath he had not asked for, and it went all the way down, all the way to the bottom, and Adam Rooke discovered at the age of thirty-eight-or-thirty that he had never in his life taken a full breath.

*This is what everyone else has.*

That was the thought, and it arrived with a kind of outrage.

*This is the baseline. This is what a fourteen-year-old on a west face gets for free at Gate One after nine years at a school, and I have been walking around for six weeks at* less *than nothing, and I thought that was what being alive felt like.*

And behind it, immediate and unwanted:

*And I got it in thirty-one hours by holding a retractor.*

He went down on one knee. He put his hand out and found the edge of the trolley and held it.

The trolley was on castors.

It moved about eighteen inches, very fast, and the tray on the top of it went off the edge and everything on the tray went across the floor of the tack room — the clamps, the packs, the two curved needles already threaded, the second set of retractors that Tolen had laid out for the next one — into the blood and the sawdust and the dirt of a floor that forty men had walked across in thirty-one hours.

The room stopped.

"Out," said Varn.

"I—"

"*Out*."

---

His name was Ollum.

He was the man who had asked four times whether anyone had gone for his wife at the Verge works, and who had said "All right" when Adam finally told him the truth, and who had come off the yard wall at hour eighteen because Wren was right about a third of them and he had been one of them.

He had been next.

He went onto the table forty minutes later instead of four, because forty minutes is what it takes to boil instruments when you have one pot and no second set, and by then the thing inside him that had been waiting had had forty minutes it should not have had.

Varn worked on him for two hours.

At the end of it she came out into the yard where Adam was sitting against the wall under the awning with his arms across his knees, and she stood in front of him, and she said:

"He's gone."

Adam did not say anything.

"I want to tell you the true version," said Ilse Varn, "because you are going to build a false one tonight and it will either be too kind or too cruel and both are useless to me."

"All right."

"The true version is that I do not know." She sat down on the step, heavily, which he had never seen her do. "He was bleeding somewhere I could not see for eleven hours. He may have died at two o'clock this afternoon and taken until tonight to finish. Or the forty minutes killed him. I have been doing this for twenty-two years and I cannot tell you which, and any surgeon who tells you they can is lying or selling something."

"But the forty minutes might have killed him."

"Yes."

"And the forty minutes were mine."

"The forty minutes were *yours*," said Varn. "Yes. That part is not in doubt."

They sat.

"I want to do the arithmetic," said Adam, after a while.

"I'd rather you didn't."

"I'm going to do it whether I say it out loud or not. I'd rather do it where someone can correct me." He turned his head. "How long does it take to boil a set?"

Varn closed her eyes.

"Thirty-five minutes to a rolling boil in that pot, from cold," she said. "Forty-two if it's been used, because you have to scrub first."

"And there was one pot."

"There was one pot because the second one cracked in the spring and forty crowns went on a pump." She said it without any edge at all, which was worse. "And before you build something out of that — the pump saved more than the pot would have. I have thought about it already. I thought about it while I was closing him."

Adam sat with his arms across his knees.

*So the sum is: a pump bought in week five kept a ward supplied through thirty-one hours and cost a second instrument set, and the missing set cost a man forty minutes on the one night in eleven years when forty minutes mattered.*

*And the forty minutes only existed because I put my hand on a trolley.*

*And I put my hand on the trolley because something in me came apart, which I did not ask for and did not cause and could not have stopped.*

He examined that last clause carefully, because it was the one that would be load-bearing for a long time, and he found that it was true and that it was also the precise shape of every excuse he had ever heard from a man across a table.

"I stepped back," he said. "I told you I needed to be replaced. I did that."

"You did."

"Fifty seconds earlier and I'd have been in the corridor."

"Yes."

"And if I'd said nothing at all I'd have been holding a clamp inside a living man when it happened, and we would be having a different conversation."

Varn opened her eyes.

"Rooke," she said. "I am going to say this once and then I am going to sleep. You are doing the thing where a clever man works out that he is technically innocent."

"I know."

"Do you."

"Yes." He looked at his hands, which were warm, which they had not been for six weeks. "I know exactly what I'm doing. I've watched forty people do it across a table and I could always see it and I have never once been able to see it from the inside until tonight."

"And?"

"And it's very comfortable," said Adam Rooke. "That's what nobody tells you. It's not a lie. Every single step of it is true. That's what makes it work."

Varn got up off the step.

"Then here is the only thing I have," she said. "It is not much and it is twenty-two years old and it is all anybody ever gets."

"Go on."

"You will not be able to decide tonight whether you killed him." She pulled her sleeves down. "So don't. Decide instead what you do on the next night like this one, because there will be one, and on that night you will know something you did not know tonight — which is that whatever this is, it can happen to you while your hands are inside a person."

She went in.

Adam sat under the awning until it started to get light.

And the thing he kept returning to — not the trolley, not the forty minutes, not the technically-innocent arithmetic — was that at some point in the eleven seconds, in the middle of the heat and the marrow and the first full breath of his life, he had felt something that he had no right to feel and could not now unfeel.

He had felt *glad*.

Not afterwards. Not with the excuse of ignorance. In the eleven seconds themselves, with a man on the table four feet away and his own hand going out towards a trolley on castors, some part of Adam Rooke had been entirely, uncomplicatedly delighted.

He put his head back against the wall and looked at the sky going grey over the ridge, where the dust had finally stopped.

*That's the thing to watch,* he thought. *Not the taking. I can refuse the taking; I refused it eleven times tonight and I would refuse it again.*

*It's that I was happy. And nothing on this wall or in that room made any difference to it at all.*

"What happened to you in there," she said.

And Adam Rooke sat in the dark with his back against the wall of Mercy House Ninth, forty-one hours awake, with a body that had stopped being cold, and made the second real decision of his second life.

"I broke through," he said.

---

She did not believe him for about ninety seconds, and then she did, entirely, and the speed of the turn told him a great deal about how rare it was and how much it mattered.

"You're thirty."

"I think so."

"Nobody breaks Tempering at thirty. It happens between eleven and nineteen or it does not happen." She was staring at him. "And it does not happen *spontaneously*. It happens after nine years of a school and a method and a master, and it happens in a hall, deliberately, with somebody standing by."

"Then I don't know what to tell you. I have been carrying water for six weeks and holding a retractor for thirty-one hours and something opened."

"Show me your hands."

He held them out.

She took them — both, turned them over, thumbs into the palms, and he watched her face change as she went up the forearms, and she stopped at the elbow and let go.

"Your marrow's changed," said Varn. "I can feel it from outside. That's — Rooke, that is a Tempering gate. That is exactly what a Tempering gate feels like and I have felt four."

"Is it visible?"

"To me. To any sensitive at four feet. To an assessor at nine." She sat back. "You have spent six weeks being invisible and you are not invisible any more, and you did it in a tack room in front of three people on the worst night this house has ever had."

Adam looked at his hands.

*And it cost me nothing,* he thought. *That is the part I will have to live with. It did not cost me a thing.*

*It cost Ollum forty minutes.*

It was not a temptation. He wanted to be entirely accurate about that, lying in the dark in a store room with his marrow changed, and he was: it was not a temptation, because a temptation is a thing you want.
