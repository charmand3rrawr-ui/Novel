# Chapter 5 — The Assumption Underneath

The Accord inspector came on the twenty-second day, and he came without notice, which Wren told Adam was the entire point of him.

"They do it three times a year and they never say when. It's supposed to stop us dressing the place up." She was moving as she said it, faster than he had seen her move, straightening a run of blankets with two passes of her hand. "Which would work, except we can't dress it up. There's nothing to dress it up *with*. So all it does is mean we're always a bit frightened."

"What does he look at?"

"Beds, drugs, the register, and the dead." She snapped a corner down. "Mostly the dead."

Adam stopped with a bucket in each hand.

"Why mostly the dead?"

"Because that's the number that follows us." Wren went on down the row. "If your dead go up, your allocation goes down. That's how it works. They'll tell you it's about standards."

He set the buckets down very slowly.

"Say that again."

"I said they'll tell you—"

"No. The first part."

She turned round.

"If the deaths go up," said Wren Halloway, "the allocation goes down. Money, drugs, Varn's Thursdays. All of it."

Adam Rooke stood in the middle of a charity ward with his hands empty and felt the whole building rearrange itself around a single sentence.

---

He found the ledgers in the afternoon, and Wren let him, because he asked directly and because by that point he had been carrying water for three weeks and had held a millwright's hand together.

There were nine years of them in a cupboard in the intake room, and they were, by the standards of anything he had ever worked with, *magnificent*. Not the handwriting, which varied from adequate to criminal. The discipline. Somebody — several somebodies, over nine years — had written down every admission, every transfer, every discharge and every death, with a date and a cause, and had never once skipped a week.

He could read perhaps one word in five.

He worked through it anyway, for six hours, with Petren beside him reading out words for a boiled sweet apiece, and by the end of it he had what he needed, and he had it because the thing he needed was not words.

It was shape.

"Petren."

"Mm."

"This column here. Fourth from the left. It's a number every time, isn't it? Never a word."

"It's the days."

"Days of what?"

"Days they were in." The boy swung his legs. "Dala says they put it in because of the Seventh thing."

"What Seventh thing?"

"When they go to Seventh."

Adam sat back on the bench.

*There it is.*

He had suspected it in the store room three weeks ago, from a table read aloud, and he had said it out loud to Varn because it was interesting. Now he had nine years of primary data and a nine-year-old with a sweet in his cheek, and he could go and get it properly.

He worked until two in the morning, and the method he used was one he invented in the first forty minutes out of pure necessity, and he was quietly proud of it for the rest of his life.

He could not read. Therefore he could not sort, could not search, could not scan a column for a term. Everything a literate man does with a page was closed to him.

What was not closed to him was *position*.

The ledgers were ruled. Every entry sat in the same place on the line every time, because whoever had ruled them nine years ago had been a person of strong opinions about ruling. The fourth column was always the fourth column. A number was always a number, because numerals were the one part of the script he had cracked in week one — there were only nine of them and Petren had thought it hilarious to test him.

So he did not read the ledgers. He *measured* them.

He took a length of twine off a bale in the store room and knotted it at intervals against the ruling, and used it to find the fourth column on any page without looking at the words, and then he went through nine years at speed, calling numbers out to himself under his breath while Petren, who had been bribed with four boiled sweets and the promise of a fifth, made a chalk mark on the intake bench for each one in a row that Adam specified.

Tally marks. Nine rows of them, one per year, and inside each row a cluster for each value of the day-count.

It took four hours and produced, on a scrubbed bench in a cold room, a histogram.

Adam stood back from it at about one in the morning and looked at nine years of a hospital drawn in chalk by a nine-year-old, and the shape was there, and it was so clear that for a second he could not breathe properly.

Every year, the left-hand side of the distribution was heavier. Every year, more of the mass sat at three days and four days and less of it out at eleven and fifteen and twenty.

People were leaving earlier. Not dying earlier. *Leaving.*

"Petren."

"I want my sweet."

"You'll get two. Tell me what this word says." He put his finger on the head of the fourth column, where it was written once at the top of each page.

"*Held*," said Petren.

"Held. Not stayed."

"Held." The boy shrugged with his whole body. "It's what Dala says. How long we held them."

Adam looked at the chalk.

*Held. They even called it the right thing. Nine years of somebody writing down exactly the correct quantity, in the correct column, on every page, because a clerk in the capital once decided the form should have it — and not one person in the chain has ever asked what the column is for.*

He sent Petren to bed at half past one, over furious objections, and paid him three sweets rather than two, which was a bribe and which he knew was a bribe.

Then he did the last hour on his own, and the last hour was the one that mattered, because it was the one where he stopped counting and started dividing — and dividing was the only part he could do entirely in his head, and he did it faster than he had ever done anything in his old life, because there was nothing else in his head to get in the way.

At two in the morning he sat back in a cold intake room with a stub of chalk and the ledger of a charity hospital, and he had it, and it was worse than he had thought.

---

He put it in front of Ilse Varn on the Thursday, forty minutes before the inspector was due, and she was furious with him for about ninety seconds.

"I have a list," she said. "I have nine patients and four hours."

"I know."

"Then whatever this is—"

"It's eleven minutes," said Adam. "And if I'm right, you've been fighting the wrong fight for eleven years. I'd rather you were angry now than in eleven more."

Varn put down the case.

He had it laid out on the table in the tack room, and because he could not write, he had done it in objects: nine years of ledgers stacked in a row, and beside each stack a line of chalk marks on the wood, and a second line under it, and a gap between the two lines that widened as it went left to right.

"Tell me what those are," she said.

"Top line is your deaths per hundred admissions, per year. Bottom line is your deaths per hundred admissions if you only count the people who were still here on day five." He looked up. "Top line's gone up every year for nine years. Bottom line's gone *down* for seven of them."

Varn looked at the chalk for a long moment.

"That can't both be true."

"It's not only both true, it's the same fact said twice." He moved along the table. "Seventh takes your stable patients on day four. Every year Seventh has taken more of them — I can see it in the fourth column, the day-count, the distribution's shifted left every year since the pump failed, which I think is when your reputation went, but that's a guess and I'd want more before I'd say it in a room."

"Go on."

"So every year, a larger fraction of the people who were going to live are removed from your denominator on day four. What's left in your beds is a population that is, by construction, sicker every year." He set the last stack down. "You are not getting worse. You are being *concentrated*. And the Accord is measuring you on a number that goes up when you're concentrated, and cutting your allocation when it goes up, which sends more of your recoverable patients to Seventh, which concentrates you further."

He stopped.

"It's a feedback loop," he said. "It has been running for nine years. It will not stop on its own, and it does not require anybody in the Accord to be malicious. It only requires them to use the ratio they use."

The extraction fan that did not work sat dead in the wall.

"Say the number," said Varn.

"Which number?"

"The one you're not saying, because you've been in this house three weeks and you think you're not allowed."

Adam Rooke looked at the stacks of nine years.

"At the current trend," he said, "Ninth closes in four years. Maybe five. Not by decision. Your allocation crosses the line where you can't staff nights, and Wren is sixty-one, and when she goes the register stops being kept properly, and a house that can't produce a register gets reclassified." He paused. "The building will still be here. There just won't be a hospital in it."

Ilse Varn sat down on the edge of the operating table.

"And the fix?" she said.

"There isn't a fix. There's an argument."

"Then give me the argument."

"You stop letting them measure your deaths per admission and you make them measure your deaths per *admission-day at risk*." He put two fingers on the table. "It's the same data. It's already in your fourth column; you've been collecting it for nine years without knowing why. It says: given the people we actually had, for the time we actually had them, how did we do. It's the only honest way to compare two houses where one is allowed to hand its recoveries to the other."

"They'll refuse."

"They might. But they can't refuse on the grounds that it's wrong, because it isn't, and they'll have to refuse on some other grounds, in writing, and the other grounds will be embarrassing." He straightened up. "That's usually enough. In my experience, an institution will do almost anything rather than write down the real reason."

Varn was very still.

"In your experience," she repeated.

Adam said nothing.

"Twenty-two days," said Ilse Varn, "and you can't read. And you've just told me the thing I have been unable to see for eleven years, in a language I have never heard anyone use, about a table I have carried to four hearings."

"Yes."

"Who *are* you?"

And Adam Rooke — who had promised himself on a road, and had already broken it twice, and who was standing in a tack room with forty minutes left before an inspector arrived — made a choice that was not an accident and did not feel like one.

"Somebody who was very good at exactly one thing," he said, "and who is going to be very good at it here, if you'll let me, and who would rather not be asked anything else for a while."

Varn looked at him for a long moment.

"Get Wren," she said.

---

The inspector's name was Coadjutor Pell and he was neither cruel nor stupid, which Adam had privately been hoping for and had prepared against.

He was about fifty, tired in a different way from Varn — tired like a man with eleven houses on his list and a vehicle that was not reliable — and he went through the ward with a board and a stylus, and he was courteous to patients, and he noted the standpipe without being told.

He got to the register at half past two.

"Deaths are up again," he said. He did not say it unkindly. "Ninth's fourth year of it, Halloway."

"Yes," said Wren.

"You know what I have to put."

"I know what you have to put."

And then Ilse Varn, who had not gone near the table, said from the doorway of the tack room:

"Before you put it. There's a man here who'd like eleven minutes of your time, and I'd take it as a favour."

Pell turned round.

He looked at Adam — the borrowed shirt, the bucket calluses that had been a liar's calluses three weeks ago and were honest ones now — and Adam watched him do the arithmetic that everyone on this world did on him, and arrive, correctly, at *nobody*.

"And who's this?"

"He's an orderly," said Wren.

"He's a mathematician," said Varn.

Adam said, "I'm a man who found a fault in your ratio, and I'd like to show you, because if I'm wrong you'll have wasted eleven minutes and if I'm right you've been closing houses for nine years by accident."

Coadjutor Pell stood with his stylus in his hand.

Then he pulled out a chair, and sat down in the middle of a charity ward, and said:

"Go on, then."

---

It took nineteen minutes, not eleven.

He did not start with the ledgers. That was the first thing he got right, and he got it right because he had watched four hundred people get it wrong across sixteen years: they start with their evidence, because their evidence is the thing they are proud of, and by the time they reach the point the listener has stopped.

He started with Pell.

"How many houses on your list?"

The coadjutor blinked. "Eleven."

"And of the eleven, how many have had rising deaths four years running?"

A pause. "Four."

"Are they the four worst houses?"

Pell opened his mouth, and stopped, and did not answer.

"I'm not being clever," said Adam. "It's a real question and you've got the answer, because you go to all eleven and nobody else does. Are the four with rising deaths the four worst houses you visit?"

"No," said Pell. "Ninth is — " He stopped again. "Ninth is not a bad house."

"Then you already know something's wrong with the measure," said Adam. "You've known it for a while. You've just had nothing to do with knowing it."

The ward was quiet. Somewhere down the row a man coughed twice and settled.

"Go on," said Pell, in a different voice.

Adam went on.

He laid it out the way he would have laid out a reserving error to a board: the mechanism first, in one sentence, with no numbers at all. *Seventh takes your recoveries on day four, so every year the people left in these beds are sicker, and a sicker population dies more, and nobody has done anything wrong.*

Then the consequence, also in one sentence. *So the ratio you are about to write down does not measure this house. It measures how much of this house has been moved somewhere else.*

Then, and only then, the chalk.

"Those are day-counts. Nine years, one row each, oldest at the top." He walked Pell along the bench. "Don't read them. Just look at where the weight sits."

Pell looked.

"It's moving," he said.

"Every year. Left every year for nine years."

"That's people going to Seventh."

"That's people going to Seventh, and every one of them is a recovery that this house produced and somebody else gets counted for." Adam straightened. "Now — and this is the part I'd want you to take to your superiors, so I'm going to give you the objection before I give you the answer."

Pell looked up sharply. "The objection?"

"Somebody in the capital is going to say: *the ratio is the same for every house, so it's fair.* That's the objection. It's a good one and it's the first thing anybody sensible will say." He put two fingers on the bench. "The answer is that the ratio is only fair between houses that transfer at the same rate, and Ninth transfers at four times the rate of Third, because Ninth is eleven miles from Seventh and Third is sixty. You are not measuring medicine. You are measuring *geography*."

There was a long silence.

Coadjutor Pell sat in a hard chair in a charity ward and looked at nine rows of chalk marks made by a nine-year-old.

"Say the last bit again," he said. "Slowly."

"You are measuring geography," said Adam, "and calling it standards."

Then he ran it a third time as the number, because a man who is going to carry an argument to his superiors needs to have done the sum himself at least once, with his own hand, or he will fold the first time somebody frowns at him.

Pell checked it on the board. It took him a long while — he was not quick, and he knew he was not quick, and he did it anyway, in front of them, without apologising for the time — and he got the same answer.

"Deaths per held-day," he said at last.

"Deaths per held-day."

"It's the same figures."

"It's the same figures. It's the fourth column and the death column and nothing else. You already collect both." Adam let that sit for a moment. "That's the part I'd lead with, if I were you. Not that the old measure is wrong. That the new one costs nobody anything and requires no new form."

At the end of it Coadjutor Pell sat back and looked at nine years of ledgers.

"I can't change the form," he said.

"No."

"The form is set in the capital and I am the eleventh man in a line who fills it in."

"I know."

"But." Pell tapped the stylus against his teeth. "There's a box on the back. Observations of the inspecting officer. It's nine lines. Nobody has ever read anything I have put in it."

"Then this will be the first thing in it that's worth reading," said Adam, "and you should write it as though somebody will, because eventually somebody does."

Pell laughed — a short, tired bark of a laugh — and turned the board over.

"Say it slowly," he said. "I write slowly."

Adam said it slowly.

And at some point during it, with a coadjutor of the Ninefold Accord writing down his words on the back of a form in a charity ward, the thing in his head spoke for the sixth time.

**INSIGHT +2**
**AUTHORITY +1**

**ASSESSMENT UPDATED.**
**SUBJECT: UNREGISTERED — FLAGGED.**

Adam Rooke did not stop talking. He did not change expression. He finished the sentence he was on, and the two after it, and he waited for Pell's stylus to catch up, and he watched the word **FLAGGED** sit in the middle of his vision above a man's bent head.

*Flagged by whom,* he thought, quite calmly.

*And under whose policy.*

---

Pell left at four. He shook Varn's hand. He did not shake Adam's, because inspectors do not shake hands with orderlies, but he looked at him on the way out for slightly too long.

Wren went to see him to the gate. Varn went back to her list, and did four procedures in ninety minutes, and did not speak.

Adam carried water.

He got four runs done before Wren came and stood in the yard gate and watched him do the fifth, and then said, "Put it down."

He put it down.

She came across the yard with her arms folded against the cold and stopped about six feet away, which he noticed, because she was a woman who normally stopped at two.

"That was the best thing anyone has ever done in this house," she said.

"It was nineteen minutes."

"Don't." She said it sharply enough that he stopped. "Don't do the thing where you make it small. I've watched three people do that in forty years and every one of them was doing it so that nobody could take it off them."

He was quiet.

"Nine years I've kept that register," Wren said. "Every night. Through two winters where we had no coal and the year my sister died and the eleven weeks I had the shaking sickness and wrote it lying down. I kept it because the form said to keep it. I have never once known what the fourth column was *for*."

"Nobody did. That's rather the point—"

"I know what the point is, I was standing there." She unfolded her arms. "I'm not asking you to explain it again. I'm telling you what it was like to stand in my own ward and find out that the thing I've been doing every night for nine years was the evidence, and I've been carrying it the whole time, and I couldn't read it either."

The standpipe dripped.

"You're angry," said Adam, carefully.

"I'm *something*." She laughed, short and without much in it. "Give me a minute, I'll tell you which when I've got it."

She went and sat down on the step — the one he had been sitting on for three weeks, the one by the standpipe — and after a moment he came and sat down too, at the far end of it, and they looked at the wall of the outbuilding opposite.

"Varn says you're staying," she said eventually.

"Varn hasn't asked me."

"Varn doesn't ask, she announces, you'll get used to it." Wren pressed her hands together between her knees. "I want to say a thing and I want to say it badly, so let me get it out in one go."

"All right."

"I've been suspicious of you since the first night. You know that. I as good as told you." She looked at the wall. "And I've decided this afternoon that I'm going to go on being suspicious of you, and that it isn't fair, and I'm going to do it anyway."

Adam turned his head.

"Why tell me?"

"Because the alternative is doing it quietly, and I've done that to a man before and he left." She was quiet for a second. "You've done a good thing today and it doesn't make you a good man and it doesn't make you a bad one. It makes you a man who did a good thing, and I've known a great many of those, and about a third of them were the worst people I ever met."

"That's a remarkable thing to say to someone who just saved your hospital."

"Yes," said Wren Halloway. "It is. I've been thinking about how to say it since three o'clock and that's the best I could do."

He sat with it.

And what he felt — he examined it carefully, because he examined everything carefully, and because this one mattered — was not offence, and was not hurt.

It was *relief*.

Somebody in this building had looked directly at him, with no malice and no fear and no debt, and had priced him correctly. Not high. Not low. *Correctly* — as an unknown with a wide distribution and a fat tail, which was exactly what he was, and which nobody, in either of his lives, had ever once said out loud to his face.

"Thank you," he said.

Wren gave him a sideways look. "For what, precisely?"

"For not deciding."

She thought about that.

"Mm," she said. "Well. Don't thank me yet. I'm sixty-one and I've been wrong four times."

She got up, with effort, and stood looking down at him for a second.

"You'll want to know what I've settled on. What I am, about this afternoon."

"You said you'd tell me when you had it."

"I've got it." She pulled her shawl up. "I'm frightened. Not of you — don't flatter yourself, I've been frightened of better men than you'll ever be. I'm frightened because I watched a man who's been here three weeks walk into a room and move an institution with his mouth, and I've spent nine years pushing at the same door with both hands."

She went to the gate.

"And I found I was glad," said Wren. "That's the bit I don't like. I was *glad*, and being glad about a thing like that is how people end up giving someone the run of the place."

She went in.

Adam Rooke sat on the step and let the light go, and he let himself look at the afternoon properly for the first time.

He had done a good thing.

That was not a rationalisation; he examined it from several angles and it held. A house that had been dying by arithmetic now had nine lines on the back of a form, and the nine lines were true, and there was a non-trivial chance — he put it at one in four, which was the most honest number he could construct — that somebody in the capital would read them within two years.

Four hundred people a year came through Mercy House Ninth. If the house stayed open eleven years longer than it would have, that was four thousand four hundred people, and some fraction of them would live who would not have.

He sat with that. He tried to feel it.

He felt, instead, the other thing — the thing that had been standing behind him since the tack room, waiting politely for the ward to empty.

*I did that with my mouth.*

Three weeks. One conversation with a tired man in a chair. No strength, no realm, no technique, no registration, no name. He had walked into a room with nine years of somebody else's handwriting and a stub of chalk, and he had changed the trajectory of an institution, and the entire cost to him had been nineteen minutes.

He turned his hands over in the dark and looked at them.

*Three units,* he thought. *Off a dying animal in a field. That's what it took to make these feel like a man's hands again, and I have been carrying that number around for three weeks like it was a fortune.*

*And this afternoon I did something worth four thousand people, and the thing in my head paid me three units for it.*

He sat very still.

Because that was the sum, wasn't it. That was the whole sum, sitting in the yard with him under two moons.

The system paid for *both*. It had paid him for a dying beast and it had paid him for a good argument, and it had paid roughly the same, and it had not once indicated a preference.

It had no view about what he was. It had only a view about whether he was *getting larger*.

And there was one more line in it, and he made himself get to the end, because a man who does not finish the sentence is simply a man who intends to think it again later with less supervision.

Sabet Orne had been worth nine.

One woman, sixty-one years old, four days from the end of a life that had included two sons and a dialect and forty years of teaching people their letters — and she had been worth three times what the beast was worth, and she had come free, and all he had had to do was stand still for fifty-one minutes and not walk down a corridor.

He had done the honest thing. He was almost sure he had done the honest thing.

But he had now, sitting on this step, done the other calculation, and it had taken him four seconds, and the four seconds had gone like this:

*Nine units for a death I did not cause, did not hasten, and could not have prevented.*

*Against nineteen minutes and nine lines on the back of a form for four thousand people.*

*And the second one is unambiguously the better act, and I will do it again, and I want to be the sort of man who does it again.*

*But the first one requires nothing of me at all.*

Adam Rooke put his head back against the cold wall of a charity hospital on a world with two moons, and understood — with the total, useless clarity that had been the great curse of both his lives — precisely what he was going to become, and approximately how long it was going to take.

"Not yet," he said, to the standpipe.

It was the third time.

He had stopped noticing that he was counting.

Above him, in the middle of his vision, where it had been sitting patiently since four o'clock while a coadjutor wrote nine lines on the back of a form, the word was still there.

**FLAGGED.**

It did not explain itself. It did not brighten, or thin, or lead anywhere with a thread of pale colour along the ground. It simply sat, the way a line in a policy sits: unremarkable, correctly formatted, and placed there by somebody who had been under no obligation whatsoever to tell him what it meant.

Adam looked at it for a long while.

Then he got up off the step, and picked up the two buckets he had put down when Wren came into the yard, and went to fill them, because the ward wanted water at six and it was twenty to.

He was still carrying it, hours later, when he finally slept: not the word, and not the fear, but the small clean fact underneath both of them, the one he had been circling all day without letting himself land.

Somewhere on this world there was a register with his name in it now.

And for the first time since a kitchen floor in another universe, Adam Rooke was on somebody's list.
