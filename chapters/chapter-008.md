# Chapter 8 — Assessment

The stall on the Quarry Road sold boiled sweets, tallow, cord, and four kinds of dried fish, and the man who ran it was called Ordren and had one arm.

Adam noticed the arm and then noticed himself noticing it, and then noticed the third thing, which was that his first thought on seeing a missing limb was no longer *what happened* but *when*.

*Four years. Maybe five. The scarring's matured and he's stopped favouring the side.*

He bought four sweets he did not want and stood at the corner in the thin morning sun and waited eleven minutes, and Merrit Oss came up the road on foot, alone, in a coat that was not grey.

"You're early," said Oss.

"I'm always early. It's not a virtue, it's an anxiety."

"Hm." Oss bought dried fish, which struck Adam as a sensible thing to do at a stall you are using for cover, and they walked up the Quarry Road together like two men who had met by accident.

"Am I being recruited?" said Adam.

"You're being *warned*, and then, if you're still standing, you may be recruited." Oss did not look at him. "I want to do the warning properly, so I'm going to ask you a question first and I need a true answer. Have you ever been assessed?"

"I don't know the word in the way you're using it."

"Then that's your answer and it's the one I expected." Oss stopped walking. "Rooke. On this world, when a person shows an aptitude that other people can't account for, there is a procedure. It is not optional. It has been not-optional for about six hundred years."

---

He laid it out in nine minutes, walking, in the flat delivery of a man who had explained it many times to people who had not wanted to hear it.

Aptitude was registered. Not cultivation — cultivation was registered separately and far more heavily — but *aptitude*, the raw fact of a person being unaccountably good at something. A child who did sums too fast. A woman who could hear what was wrong with an engine. A man who could look at a page he could not read and tell you where the lie was.

The Ninefold Accord ran the register. The Accord had run it since the Accord was nine trading houses and a grievance, and the reason it ran it was not sinister and Oss said so twice.

"It's insurance," he said. "That's genuinely what it is. Four hundred years ago the thin places started appearing and nobody knew why, and it turned out one in eleven of them was a single gifted person drawing too hard in one spot for thirty years. So they started counting. And once you are counting a thing you are administering it, and once you are administering it you are deciding who gets to do it, and here we are."

"And what happens at an assessment?"

"You go to a hall. Three assessors and a clerk. It takes a day." Oss kicked a stone off the road. "They test the aptitude, they grade it, they enter you in the register, and they issue papers. Real papers. Parish, house of origin, the lot — the register creates a person where there wasn't one, which is why it is the only route to papers available to a man in your position."

Adam walked for a while.

"And the cost."

"Two costs. The first is that you are then in the register, and the register is consulted." Oss counted them off. "The second is that a graded aptitude carries a duty. They can direct you. Not often, and not arbitrarily, and the pay is decent — but a Grade Four appraiser who is told to go and appraise something goes and appraises it, or the papers stop."

"Consulted by whom?"

"By anyone with standing to ask. Houses. Sects. The Verge Wardens, constantly." Oss shrugged. "It's not a secret list. That's rather the point of it — it exists so that when a thin place opens somebody can find out in an afternoon who's been living within nine miles of it for thirty years."

Adam walked a few paces.

"So it's a search index."

"It's a *register*."

"It's a search index," said Adam, "maintained at public expense, cross-referenced by location and aptitude, and consultable by any institution with standing. You've described the mechanism and then used the word that makes it sound like a filing cabinet."

Oss opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"That," he said after a moment, "is exactly the sort of thing that is going to get you detained, and I would keep it behind your teeth."

"I'm not being clever at you. I'm trying to price it." Adam stopped. "If I go into that hall I am buying papers, and the price is that I become permanently findable by every organisation on this continent that has ever wanted to find someone like me. And you're asking me to pay it in advance, on trust, before I know what I am."

"Yes," said Merrit Oss. "That's precisely what I'm asking. I'd like to pretend it's better than that and it isn't."

"How often is not often?"

"For a Four? Twice a year. For a One—" Oss stopped himself.

"Go on."

"There hasn't been a Grade One assessed in nineteen years," said Merrit Oss, "and I have read his file, and he does not have a life. He has a schedule."

---

They walked as far as the crossroads and Adam did not say anything for most of it, which Oss allowed, which Adam noted and filed as either courtesy or technique.

"You want me assessed," he said eventually.

"I want you assessed and graded and put in the register where I can point at you," said Oss, "because the alternative is that in about four months somebody in the grey notices an unregistered man with no parish is the source of a structural reform, and that person will not be me, and they will not come with dried fish."

"And then?"

"And then it stops being about the reform." He said it without drama. "You'd be detained. Not violently, not illegally. There's a form. You'd be held at a hall for assessment and assessed under compulsion, and the difference between that and walking in yourself is roughly the difference between negotiating a price and being told one."

Adam stopped at the crossroads.

He stood there with his hands in the pockets of a coat that had been given to him and thought, with the cold clean clarity he had begun to be frightened of:

*He is entirely right and it is a good offer and I cannot take it.*

"Can I ask what I'd be graded on?"

"Whatever you can demonstrate. They'll give you tasks."

"And if the thing I'm good at isn't in their list?"

"Then they'll grade the nearest thing." Oss shrugged. "It's not exact. It's a bureaucracy."

*No,* thought Adam. *It's a measuring instrument, and I have spent a month learning that the thing you must never do, on this world or the last one, is let somebody measure you with an instrument whose calibration you have not seen.*

Because they would not test the thing he actually had. They could not; nobody on this planet knew it existed, and he had a growing suspicion that this was not an accident.

They would test him on sums and pages and pattern, and he would score extremely well, and they would enter *Rooke, Adam — appraisal aptitude, Grade Four* in a book.

And then — this was the part that arrived last and sat worst — a man in a hall would put his hand on Adam's chest, or a wand, or whatever instrument they used, to check what he was drawing and from where. And the thing in his head had spoken to him six times and had never once explained itself, and had changed his status to **FLAGGED** on the afternoon he made himself visible, and had shown him the word **OBSERVED** while a stranger stood at the gate and wrote something down.

*If they can detect it, I am finished on the day of the assessment.*

*If they cannot detect it, then there is something on this world that their six-hundred-year-old register cannot see, and I am carrying it, and I have no idea what it is for.*

He found he had been standing at the crossroads for some time.

And underneath all of it, in the place where he kept the things he had not yet agreed to know, a fifth consideration sat and waited for him to pick it up.

*They have a register of people who are unaccountably good at things. It has run for six hundred years. It exists because gifted people draw too hard in one spot and make thin places.*

*Which means the Accord's entire apparatus is built on one assumption: that a gifted person* draws*.*

*That the essence goes* into *them from the world, and that you can find them by measuring what's gone.*

He turned it over.

*And I do not draw.*

He had never once felt the ground go thin under him. He had absorbed exactly twice — a beast in a field and, in every sense that mattered, a decision in a corridor — and neither time had anything come out of the world. It had come off a body. It had come off something that had finished with it.

*I am not a drain. I'm a scavenger.*

*And their instrument measures drains.*

He stood at the crossroads with a parcel-less coat and four sweets in his pocket and understood, with a clarity that made the back of his neck prickle, that he might be entirely invisible to the only detection system this world had ever built — not because he was hiding, but because six hundred years ago somebody had written down the wrong definition, and every instrument since had been calibrated to it.

It should have been the best news he had received on this planet.

*Then why,* he thought, *did the thing in my head change my status to FLAGGED on the exact afternoon I made myself visible to a man with a form?*

*Flagged by whom. Flagged in what.*

*There are two registers.*

He did not know that. He had four observations and no control and he would have laughed at anyone who quoted him a view. But he stood at the crossroads and found that he believed it anyway, the way he had believed the three betrayals were a trend, and he recognised the feeling and did not trust it and could not put it down.

"You've gone somewhere," said Oss.

"I'm deciding."

"Take four days. Genuinely." Oss adjusted the parcel of fish under his arm. "I'll tell you the last part and then I'll go, because I've a train and you've a ward. The last part is the only reason I came in person."

"Go on."

"The four explanations," said Merrit Oss. "I've settled on one, and it isn't the third. You weren't placed here. I checked — properly, six ways, and there's nothing." He looked at Adam directly for the first time since the stall. "Which leaves the fourth, and I don't like the fourth, and I have decided to help you anyway."

"Why?"

"Because eleven houses get more money in the spring," said Oss, "and because I am thirty-four and I have been in the grey for eleven years and yours is the first thing I've seen that would actually move." He turned to go. "And because a man who cannot read found it, and I would like to live in a world where that man gets papers instead of a form."

---

Adam walked back down the Quarry Road at about noon with four sweets in his pocket he did not want, and he was two hundred yards from the gate when he heard it.

It was not loud. That was the thing he remembered afterwards, and it was the thing that everyone who was there remembered: it was not loud at all. It was a sound like a door closing in a very large house, a long way off, and the ground moved under his feet by perhaps a quarter of an inch, once.

He stopped.

Behind him, up the Quarry Road, the dust was already going up over the ridge.

---

He was in the yard when the first of them came down the road, and by then Wren was already in the gateway with her sleeves up.

"Quarry," she said. "West face."

"How many work it?"

"Two hundred on a day shift, and there's a second face on the east they opened in the spring, so call it two forty." Wren said it flatly, the way she said everything that had a number in it. "West face is the old workings. It's where they put the ones who can't get taken on anywhere else."

"Unlicensed?"

"Unlicensed, unregistered, and about a third of them under sixteen." She was already rolling her sleeves. "Nobody's going to come for them from Second. That's not cruelty, it's paperwork — Second takes on chargeable admissions and none of that lot is chargeable to anyone."

Adam looked up the road at the dust.

*None of them chargeable. So Seventh's capitation doesn't apply either — they'd be taking heads that nobody pays for.*

*Which means Seventh will send late, and light, and they'll take the ones with an employer.*

He did not say it. There was no version of saying it that helped, and Wren had almost certainly worked it out eleven years before he arrived. She was watching the road. "Ninth is the nearest house by four miles. Second is eleven, Seventh is nine, and they've both got the beds and we have not."

Adam looked up the road at the dust.

He had been on this world for thirty-nine days and he had worked out, in that time, approximately how everything in Mercy House Ninth functioned, and standing in the gateway he ran the whole thing in about six seconds, the way he had once run a four-hundred-page file in an afternoon.

*Forty-one beds. Thirty-three occupied. Eight free.*

*One surgeon, and it is Tuesday, and Varn is eleven miles away at Fourth.*

*Two orderlies, one intake officer, one boy, and me.*

*Dressing stock for eleven days of ordinary use.*

"Wren."

"I know."

"Wren, we've got eight beds."

"I *know*, Adam." She did not take her eyes off the road. "Get the long room cleared of anything that isn't a person. Every table, every bench, every board you can lay flat. Petren to the Quarry Road to flag for Varn's car — she'll come through there and she'll not know."

"Second and Seventh will send—"

"Second and Seventh will send in three hours and it takes two hours to get here." Wren turned round and looked at him and her face was entirely calm, which frightened him more than anything he had seen since a kitchen. "The three hours is ours. Everything that happens in the three hours happens because of what you and I do in the next four minutes."

---

He had never in his life been given an instruction that mattered, and he discovered, in the first hour, that he was very good at them and that this was not a comfort.

The four minutes went like this.

He did not start with the long room, which was what Wren had told him to do, and he had about a second and a half to decide whether to disobey her and he took it.

He went to the intake room first and got the ledger, the current one, and the pen, and he put them on the bench by the gate where Wren was standing and said, "Names as they come through. I'll do it if you can't."

She looked at him for half a beat.

"Why."

"Because in four hours somebody is going to ask this house how many it took and when, and if we can't say, Second will say it for us." He was already moving. "And because their families will come and somebody has to be able to tell them."

"Go," said Wren.

Then the long room.

He took the two trestles out of the laundry first because they were the only surfaces that were already the right height and required nothing. Then the intake bench. Then he went round the ward with the screwdriver and took four doors off their hinges — the store room, the laundry, the dry press, and the door of the room he slept in — and he did the last one without a flicker, and he noticed that he did not hesitate, and he noticed it in the way you notice a step that is not there.

The doors went on crates. The crates were the crates the winding cloth came in, which was a thought he had and immediately put down.

Then the water.

Then — and this was the one that mattered, and it was the one nobody had asked for — he went down the long room and moved eleven people.

He did it fast and he did it by the ledger in his head, and he picked the eleven who could be moved: the ones at four days and better, the stable ones, the ones whose day-count said they were going up. He put them along the west wall on the floor with their own blankets, two of them helping, one of them furious, and he apologised to each of them in a flat courteous voice and did not stop moving.

It gave him eleven more cots.

Wren found out about it forty minutes later, in the middle of everything, and she stopped dead in the middle of the ward.

"You moved my patients onto the floor."

"Yes."

"Adam—"

"They're the eleven who'd have gone to Seventh on the fourth day," said Adam. "Every one of them. I checked the counts before I touched anybody. If Seventh had taken them last week the way they take them every week, those cots would already be empty and we'd have had them anyway."

Wren Halloway stood in the middle of her own ward with blood to the elbow and looked at him.

"That is a horrible piece of reasoning," she said.

"It's the correct piece of reasoning."

"I know it is." She was already moving again. "That's what's horrible about it. Don't do it again without telling me and don't ever stop being able to do it."

---

He cleared the long room in nine minutes. He took the intake bench, the two trestles from the laundry, four doors off their hinges with a screwdriver and a brick, and laid them on crates at working height, and it gave them eleven more surfaces.

He set the water. The pump — the pump he had bought with a column width and a man's quiet embarrassment thirty-nine days after arriving on a planet — ran clear at four gallons a minute into the long room, and it meant that from the first casualty to the last nobody had to leave the ward to fetch anything, and Adam Rooke stood with his hand on that tap for a second and had a thought he was not proud of.

*I did that. Nineteen days ago. And it is going to matter more in the next four hours than everything else I have done here put together.*

Then he stopped thinking about it, because the first cart came through the gate.

---

The first cart came through the gate at twenty past one and there were six men on it and one of them was already dead, and that was the moment the day stopped being a problem and became a thing that was happening.

Adam had been braced for noise. There was almost none. The carter did not shout; he stopped the animal and got down and stood beside it with his hands open, waiting to be told what to do, and the men on the cart made very little sound at all, and the quiet of it was the worst thing Adam had ever heard.

He went to the tailgate because there was nobody else at the tailgate.

The dead one was on the outside and he was about forty and there was grey dust in every line of his face, and the dust had gone to paste around his mouth where somebody had tried to give him water.

Adam stood with his hand on the tailgate and waited for the words.

Nothing came. No source, no thread, no time to residue.

*Too late. He's been gone half an hour.*

And it was a *relief* — that was the thing that went through him first, ahead of anything else, a clean flood of relief that this man had died in a cart on a road and not in front of him — and Adam Rooke registered the relief and understood exactly what it implied about the next six hours, and then put it down and got to work, because there were five others and one of them was trying to get off the cart by himself.

"Sit down," he said. "Sit down. I've got you. What's your name?"

"Corrin."

"Corrin, I'm Adam, I'm going to take your weight on this side. Don't help me."

That was the first one. There were a hundred and nine more before midnight.

---

Triage was Wren's and she did it standing in the gateway with her hand on each of them as they came past, and Adam understood within about ninety seconds that he was watching something he could not do.

She was not assessing injury. She was assessing *trajectory* — three seconds a man, a hand on the chest or the throat, and then a word, and the word sent them left or right, and the left ones went into the long room and the right ones went along the wall in the yard under the awning.

Nobody said out loud what the right-hand line was.

"Rooke." She did not look up. "Yard line. Keep them warm, keep them wet, keep them talked to. If one of them changes, shout."

"How will I know if one of them changes?"

"You'll know."

He went to the yard line.

There were four of them when he started and there were nineteen by the middle of the afternoon, and he moved up and down the line with water and blankets and the flat courteous voice that had been his mask for thirty-eight years and which turned out, in a yard, on the worst day in the history of Mercy House Ninth, to be the single most useful thing he owned.

He worked out the system in the first twenty minutes and then ran it without deviation for six hours, and the system was this.

*Water first, always, because a man who is given water believes he is being treated.*

*Warmth second. Not comfort — temperature. Half of them were going cold and cold kills quietly while everyone is looking at blood.*

*Then position. Get the ones who can be sat up sat up, because a man lying flat in a line of men lying flat starts to understand what the line is, and a man sitting up does not.*

*Then talk, and talk about something with a future in it.*

That last one he worked out by accident, in the first hour, and then applied deliberately for the rest of the day, and it was the closest thing to medicine he performed.

He had been telling Corrin about the pump — because it was the only thing in his head and because Corrin's leg was open to the bone and Adam had needed somewhere to put his own eyes — and he had got as far as the column widths on the invoices when he realised that Corrin's breathing had come down, all the way down, and stayed there.

Not because of the pump. Because there was a story with a beginning and a middle and it had not finished yet, and a man will hold on to the end of a sentence with both hands if you give him one long enough.

So he told them about the pump. Nineteen times, across six hours, with variations. He told a man called Sten the entire history of Bern Kassick's short measure and it took eleven minutes and by the end of it Sten was arguing with him about whether he should have asked for fifty crowns instead. He told Ollum about the endowment and the forty-one beds and the woman two hundred and ten years dead whose name nobody could pronounce, and Ollum said, with his eyes shut, "She'd be cross," and Adam said, "She'd be furious," and Ollum laughed and then stopped laughing because of his ribs.

And somewhere in the fourth hour Wren went past him in the gateway, carrying, and said without stopping:

"Whatever you're doing on that wall, keep doing it. I've had two come back off the line and neither of them should have."

---

He did not, in six hours, once look at a man on that wall and think about what the man was worth.

He checked afterwards. He went back over it in his head that night, deliberately, the way he had once gone back over a valuation, looking for the place where the wrong assumption had got in — and it was not there.

For six hours he had been entirely occupied, and the thing in his head had been silent because nobody had crossed the hour, and Adam Rooke had been, without noticing it and without any effort whatsoever, simply a man carrying water to people who needed water.

It was the last six hours of his life in which that would be true, and he did not know it, and there was nobody to tell him.

He learned their names because he could not stop himself. Corrin. Baeth, who was eighteen. A man called Ollum who had a wife at the Verge works and asked four times whether anyone had gone for her, and the answer was no, and Adam said "They've been told" three times and then on the fourth time said "No. Nobody has gone. There's no one to send and I'm sorry," and Ollum said "All right" and shut his eyes, and that was better, and Adam did not know why it was better and filed it.

And the words did not come.

That was the part he could not get past. Nineteen men along a wall under an awning, and some of them were going to die, and Wren had put them there precisely because she knew which — and the thing in his head said nothing at all, for four hours, because none of them was inside the last hour yet.

He worked the line, and at some point in the fourth hour he found he was doing two things at once: he was holding a man's hand and telling him about the pump, and he was counting.

*Nineteen on the wall. Wren's right-hand line is not a death sentence, it's a priority, and about a third will come back off it if Varn arrives.*

*So call it twelve.*

*Twelve at nine to fourteen units.*

He stopped in the middle of the yard with a blanket in his hands.

Adam Rooke knelt on the flagstones of a charity hospital yard holding the hand of an eighteen-year-old, with a number hanging in the air above him, and behind him along the wall were eighteen more men, and up the road the dust was still going up over the ridge, and there were four more carts coming that nobody had counted yet.

He was going to be in this yard for another nine hours.

And somewhere in the middle of those nine hours, he understood — not as a fear, not as a temptation, but as an arithmetic certainty, the way he had understood eleven months on a kitchen floor — that he was going to be offered this number eleven or twelve more times before dawn.

And that nobody in the world would ever know which way he answered.

Adam Rooke stood in the yard of a charity hospital with nineteen men along the wall and read the first of the numbers, and above the ridge the dust was still going up, and somewhere down the road there were four more carts coming that nobody had counted yet.
