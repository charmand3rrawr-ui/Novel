# Chapter 3 — None Turned Away

The woman at the intake desk did not ask him his name for eleven minutes, and when she finally did, it was almost an afterthought.

She asked him four other things first, in an order Adam found immediately and professionally interesting.

Could he walk unaided. Was he bleeding. Had he come from the east road. Did he have anything on him that would burn.

He answered yes, no, yes, and — after a pause in which he mentally inventoried a harness and a laced shirt — no.

She wrote none of it down. She was about sixty, with grey hair cut off at the jaw and a face that had been doing this a long time, and she looked at him while he answered rather than at the ledger, which meant the ledger was not the instrument. He was the instrument. She was reading him and the questions were just a way of holding him still while she did it.

He recognised the technique because he had used a version of it himself for sixteen years, and he felt, for the first time since the field, something that was almost comfort.

"Sit there," she said, and pointed with two fingers at a bench along the wall. "Don't sleep."

"Is there a reason I shouldn't sleep?"

"There's a reason I don't want you doing it on my bench."

He sat on the bench.

---

The room was forty feet long and had been built as something else.

That was obvious from the roof beams, which were too heavy, and the way the windows sat too high in the wall, and the iron rings still set into one of the pillars at waist height. A warehouse, or a stable, or a customs hall. Somebody had put a floor in it and painted the walls to about shoulder height in a green that had gone the colour of old soup, and had run a double row of cots down the length of it, and had strung electric light along the beams on a cable that was stapled rather than conduited.

Forty-one cots. He counted them twice because the first count had been eyes-only and he did not trust eyes-only.

Thirty-three were occupied.

That was the first real number he had acquired on this world, and he turned it over on the bench like a coin. Thirty-three of forty-one. An eighty per cent occupancy rate at four in the morning in a charity house is not a crisis; a crisis looks like a hundred and ten per cent and people on the floor. Eighty per cent is a *baseline*. Eighty per cent means this is what Tuesday looks like.

He sat on the bench in a dead man's shirt and did the arithmetic on a building full of the dying, and did not stop himself, because he had tried once in the field and found that stopping himself was not among the available options.

*Thirty-three beds. Call the median stay ten days, which is a guess, and I'll know inside a week whether it's a bad one. Call the mortality rate one in four, which is generous for a place that takes people nobody else will take.*

*That's eight a month. Two a week.*

He looked at the cable stapled to the beams and made himself finish it.

*Two a week, at three to four units each if a man yields what a draft animal yields, which he almost certainly does not — a man should yield more or the whole thing makes no sense — and I have been in this world for six hours and I have already worked out the annual throughput of a charity hospital.*

He put his hands flat on his knees.

"Right," he said, very quietly, to the green wall.

He did not decide anything on that bench. He wanted, later, to be able to say that he had — that there had been a moment, a fork, a place where a better man would have got up and walked out into the dark. There was not. The truth was smaller and worse: he sat on a bench for forty minutes with a sum in his head, and the sum did not horrify him, and the only thing he felt about the absence of horror was a mild professional interest in what it indicated.

---

"Name."

The grey-haired woman had come back with a tin cup and a heel of bread on a square of cloth, and she put both down on the bench beside him without ceremony.

"Adam."

"Adam what?"

"Rooke."

She had started writing before he finished, and stopped. "Spell it."

He opened his mouth and closed it again.

There was a pause of about two seconds which was, he thought afterwards, one of the worst of his life.

"I can't," he said.

She looked up.

He had rehearsed six versions of this on the road, and all six of them were lies, and every one of them had assumed he would have more information than he had. He abandoned all six.

"I can't read or write," he said. "I don't know where I am. I don't know what the date is. I woke up in a field about four miles east of here and I don't know how I got there, and I've been walking since."

He kept his hands flat on his knees and his voice level, and he did not look away from her, because looking away is what people do when they are constructing.

"I'm not ill and I'm not hurt. I've got a harness that isn't mine, off a dead beast in a field, which you're welcome to, and I have no money, and I don't know anybody. I'd like to work. I'll do whatever you want doing, including the parts nobody wants, and I'd like a place to sleep that isn't a field."

The woman put her pen down.

"You've told me four things I didn't ask," she said, "and you've told them to me in the right order, which nobody does. Who were you before?"

"I don't know."

"Mm," she said, in a tone that meant she had heard *I don't know* several thousand times and had developed a taxonomy for it. She picked the pen back up. "Rooke. R-O-O-K-E?"

"I don't—"

"It's a bird. I'm spelling it like the bird." She wrote it. "You'll learn your own name inside a week or you'll be the only person in this house who can't, and that includes Petren, who is nine and has a hole in his heart."

"Is that an offer of employment?"

"It's an offer of a mattress, and the mattress is conditional." She turned the ledger round and pushed it across with the pen. "Make a mark. Any mark. It's not a signature, it's a promise you'll answer to it."

He looked at the line she was indicating, and at the pen, and made a small precise cross.

"Wren Halloway," she said. "I run intake, nights. Don't call me sister, I'm not one. Don't call me doctor, I'm certainly not one of those." She took the ledger back. "Eat the bread. Not the cup, that's for water and you'll be wanting it in about an hour when you understand how tired you are."

---

He did not sleep until nine in the morning and by then he had carried thirty buckets.

The Mercy House drew its water from a standpipe in the yard and moved it by hand, which struck Adam as an extraordinary thing in a building with electric light, until a boy explained to him — slowly, the way you explain to the simple — that the house had a pump and the pump had failed in the spring and the part cost forty crowns and forty crowns was three months of what they had.

He thought about that for the length of a bucket run.

*Electric light on a stapled cable, and no pump. So the lighting was donated or inherited and the maintenance isn't funded. Somebody built this place with money and nobody is keeping it with money.*

It was the same shape as a hundred schemes he had priced. Endowment set up, endowment fixed in nominal terms, ninety years of inflation, and now a building with a good roof and no working anything.

He carried buckets until his shoulders were on fire and then he carried nine more, because he had worked out that the person who stops at the point of pain and the person who stops at the point of failure are treated very differently by everyone watching, and because he wanted to be treated the second way by the end of his first morning.

Somewhere in the middle of it he noticed the words.

They had come back — not summoned, not on request. They came up at the edge of his vision while he was setting a bucket down, and they were brief, and they were entirely unlike the ones in the field.

He stood in the yard with his hands on his knees and read it twice and then it was gone.

He went and sat on the step by the standpipe and put his head down and worked.

*Not absorption. Nothing died. Nobody shed anything.*

*Which means there's a second mechanism, and the second mechanism is —*

He straightened up.

*— just work. That's just work. That's what happens to anybody who carries thirty buckets. It's telling me about something that would have happened anyway.*

And then, one layer down, in the cold place:

*Or it's telling me it happened faster than it should have. And I have absolutely no way to know which, because I don't have a control.*

He filed it. It went into the same growing file as the units and the scale and the rate of decay, a file that had been open for nine hours and was already, he noted, the largest single object in his mind.

---

The woman in cot nine caught him looking at the ward board on the third afternoon.

The board hung by the door — a slate with forty-one lines ruled on it, a name against each occupied cot, and a column of short marks beside the names that changed daily. He had been standing in front of it for some time with a bucket in each hand, doing what he had been doing every day since he arrived, which was trying to break it.

Not the board. The *script*. He had been attacking it the way he would have attacked an undocumented data feed: find the delimiter, find the repeated element, find the thing that must be a date because it increments.

"You're at that again," said the woman in cot nine.

He set the buckets down. "I'm not making progress."

"No, you're not, because you're doing it like a man trying to pick a lock." She shifted herself up the pillow, which cost her something. "Bring it here."

"It's screwed to the wall."

"Then bring me a slate, boy, and stop being clever at me."

He was thirty years old in this body and had been thirty-eight in the last one and nobody had called him boy in twenty-five years, and he found that he did not mind it at all.

He brought a slate.

Her name was Sabet Orne. She had a face that had been out of doors for sixty years and hands that had done something repetitive and hard, and she taught him the way people teach who have taught children — which is to say, without any interest whatsoever in whether he found it humiliating.

"That one," she said, drawing it. "That's the front of your name. Ah. Like the start of a shout."

"A."

"Don't say it like that. You're saying it like you're agreeing with me. *Ah.*"

"Ah."

"Better. Now that one's a gate, see, two posts and a rail — that's a *d*, and it does what it looks like, it stops you." She drew a third. "And that's the one that'll give you trouble."

"Why?"

"Because it's got two sounds and nobody's ever agreed which." She drew it again, harder, so the chalk squeaked. "*R.* In the north it's a growl. Down here it's barely there. You'll hear men say the same word four ways between here and the coast and every one of them thinks the other three are foreigners."

He wrote it. It came out wrong.

"You're holding the chalk like a knife," she said.

"How should I hold it?"

"Like a chalk."

He laughed. It came out of him before he had decided anything about it — a real one, short and surprised, the first since a kitchen — and Sabet Orne looked extremely pleased with herself and pretended not to be.

They got eleven letters by the end of that afternoon. She made him write each one nine times, and then made him write them in a different order, and when he got the growling one wrong for the fifth time she said "Again," in a voice that had no sympathy in it at all, and he did it again, and got it.

"You learn fast," she said, at the end, when the light had gone amber through the high windows. "That's not a compliment. Fast learners get lazy, because they've never had to want it."

"I've wanted things."

"Mm." She looked at him sideways. "You've *calculated* things. It's not the same, and you'll find that out."

He did not have an answer for that either, which was becoming a pattern with her, and he carried the slate back and did not think about it again until it was much too late to say anything.

---

He met the surgeon on the fourth day, and the surgeon did not meet him.

Her name was Ilse Varn and she came on Thursdays, and the entire building changed shape for it. Wren had the long room cleared by seven. Two of the orderlies who normally moved like people wading came in early and moved like people who had been shouted at before and intended not to be again.

She arrived at eight in a hired flatbed with a case and two apprentices, and she went down the ward at a speed Adam initially misread as arrogance and then, watching more carefully, understood to be triage: she was spending eleven seconds on some and ninety on others and the allocation was not random.

He watched her from the end of the room with a mop in his hands, and he did not move, and over four hours he learned more than he had learned in the previous four days.

She worked in a theatre they had made out of the old tack room, and the door stayed open because the extraction fan had gone the way of the pump. From the end of the corridor he could see about a third of the table.

What she did with her hands was, as far as he could tell, ordinary surgery. Good ordinary surgery. Clean, fast, unhesitating, with the economy of someone who has done a thing eight thousand times.

What she did with her other hand was not.

She would stop — mid-procedure, no warning — and lay the back of her left hand flat against the patient's sternum or their flank or the side of their throat, and hold it there for two or three seconds with her eyes somewhere else entirely, and then go back in and change what she was doing.

Once she stopped, laid her hand on a man's chest, said something short to an apprentice, and closed him up without finishing. The man went back to a cot. He was dead before evening.

The rest of it he catalogued as he mopped, in the order he saw it, because the order he saw it in was the only structure he had.

The table was steel and had a frame over it carrying three lamps on jointed arms, and the lamps were the same electric as the beams, and one of them flickered and nobody fixed it. Beside the table, on a trolley, there was a box the size of a bread bin with a cracked enamel face and a needle behind glass, and a cable running from it to a cuff, and the cuff went round the patient's upper arm. The needle moved when the patient's chest moved. An apprentice watched the needle and said numbers out loud at intervals, and Varn did not acknowledge the numbers, and Adam understood after the third patient that she did not need them and the apprentice was being trained rather than consulted.

There was a second box on the same trolley with no face at all, just a dull grey housing and a fitting on top, and a line ran from it to a spike that went into the patient's forearm. Fluids. There was a bag hanging above it that was not glass and not quite plastic, and the drip in the line was regulated by a clamp a human being had to turn.

So: sterile steel, electric light, a mechanical sphygmomanometer, and fluid by gravity and hand-clamp. A theatre that would have been recognisable in 1940 and comprehensible in 1900.

And on the wall, screwed at eye height beside the door, a flat panel eighteen inches across with a dark glass face, dead and dusty, with four characters etched into the bezel.

He looked at that panel for a long time.

Because it was not 1940. The panel was not a thing a 1940 theatre had. Somebody had put it there, and somebody had stopped paying for whatever made it work, and it had gone dark, and the theatre had carried on without it — the same way the ward carried on without the pump, the same way the light hung on a stapled cable, the same way an endowment fixed in nominal terms in a good century becomes, in a worse one, a building with a roof and nothing else.

*This place is not primitive,* he thought, leaning on the mop. *It is* degraded*. Those are completely different diagnoses and everybody who walks in here makes the first one.*

It mattered. He turned it over while his hands did the floor. It mattered because if the world had never had the panel, then the panel was a thing he would have to invent, and inventing things takes a lifetime and a laboratory and a name people trust.

But if the world *had* the panel, and this building simply could not pay for it, then the gap between Mercy House Ninth and whatever a wealthy house looked like was not a gap in knowledge.

It was a gap in money.

And money was the one thing on this planet that Adam Rooke already knew, in his bones, exactly how to think about.

Adam mopped a corridor for four hours and thought about the back of a hand on a sternum.

It was information she was getting. It was not a pulse — she had a pulse from the wrist like anyone. It was *interior* information, arriving faster than instruments, and she trusted it enough to abandon a procedure on the strength of it.

*So that's the second ladder, applied. Not a woman walking at forty miles an hour. A woman who can read the inside of a body with the back of her hand.*

He leaned on the mop.

*And if I could do that,* he thought, *I would be the most valuable object in this building inside a month.*

---

That night he ran experiments on himself, in the dark, on a mattress in a store room, and got four results and one of them frightened him.

He had worked out the shape of the problem on the fourth bucket run of the fourth day. He had a mechanism he could not summon, could not query, and did not understand, and which had spoken to him exactly four times in six days. Four observations. No controls. No units.

In his old life he would have refused to say anything at all on four observations. He had once made an entire room of senior people wait three weeks for a number because the sample was thin, and had been right to.

He did not have three weeks. He had a mattress and a dark room and his own body.

*Experiment one. Does it respond to intent.*

He lay on his back and thought, in the most deliberate way he could manage: *status.* Then *assessment.* Then, feeling foolish, the words he had seen — *ASSESSMENT PENDING* — pronounced internally in what he imagined to be a commanding tone.

Nothing. He had expected nothing; he had run it anyway, because you run the null.

*Experiment two. Does it respond to the body.*

He did press-ups against the floor until his arms failed, which took ninety-one of them and surprised him badly — the body he had woken in could not have done thirty on day one. Nothing appeared. He lay on his face on the boards and breathed and waited a full two minutes.

Nothing.

*So it isn't effort. It fired in the yard on the thirtieth bucket and it hasn't fired since, through four days of heavier work.*

He rolled onto his back and looked at the ceiling and revised.

*Not effort. A threshold. Something crossed a line in the yard on day one and the line is further away now.*

That was worth more than the press-ups had cost. He wrote it in his head under *endurance*, and then, because he had no paper, wrote it again, and then a third time, which was a technique Sabet had taught him for letters four hours ago and which he had immediately generalised without telling her.

*Experiment three. Range.*

This was the one that mattered and he knew it going in.

He got up and went down the corridor to the long room and stood in the doorway. Thirty-two people asleep. At least four of them, by his own crude reckoning, within a month of dying.

He stood in the doorway for perhaps ninety seconds.

Nothing appeared. No thread, no line of type, no source count.

He walked the length of the ward slowly, between the cots, and stopped at the foot of each of the four he had privately marked, and stood there in the dark like a man checking a gauge, and there was nothing, and nothing, and nothing, and nothing.

He went back to the store room and sat on the mattress with his back against the wall.

*So it is not a sense. I cannot scan. It does not show me the dying.*

He turned it over for a long while, and when he got to the end of it he did not like where he was standing.

*It showed me the beast at four hundred yards through a hedge, when the beast had six minutes left. It has shown me nothing here, tonight, in a room with thirty-two people in it, four of whom are dying.*

*Therefore it is not range. It is* proximity in time*. It does not tell me who is dying. It tells me who is* about to stop*, and it tells me somewhere inside the last hour.*

He sat with that.

It meant he could not hunt. It meant he had no early warning, no list, no way to be efficient about any of this — and he noticed the word *efficient* arrive in his own thinking, unprompted, wearing ordinary clothes, and he did not enjoy noticing it.

It also meant something else, and this was the fourth result, and it was the one that frightened him.

It meant the thing in his head was not an instrument he was using.

An instrument you point. An instrument answers when asked. This did not answer, could not be aimed, and volunteered nothing — except when something near him was within an hour of the end, at which point it spoke up, unbidden, with a number attached.

*It isn't a tool,* thought Adam Rooke, on a mattress in a store room at three in the morning. *It's an appetite. It only ever talks to me about food.*

He lay down. He did not sleep for a long time, and when he did, he dreamed about the green jug, and woke up angry in a way he could not attach to anything.

On the sixth night a woman in cot nine began to die, and Adam Rooke was the only person in the room.

Her name was Sabet Orne. She was sixty-one and she had come in with something in the lungs that Wren had described, in the flat shorthand of a woman who had stopped saying the long words years ago, as *the grey*. She had two sons somewhere south. She had been in cot nine for nine days and she had liked Adam because he was new and did not talk to her the way the orderlies did, and she had spent a considerable portion of Tuesday teaching him eleven letters.

He had learned eleven letters from a dying woman on a Tuesday and had been able to write half his own name by Wednesday.

At ten past two he was doing the round with the water jug, and he got to nine, and she was awake, and the sound of her breathing had changed.

He knew it had changed. He did not know how he knew. He set the jug down.

The words came up.

It took fifty-one minutes.

He stayed for all of it. He fetched Wren at the twenty-minute mark because Sabet asked him to the second time, properly, and Wren came and sat on the other side and held the other hand and did not once look at Adam, and the three of them were there in the dark at the end of the long room with thirty-two other people asleep or pretending to be.

Sabet Orne said her sons' names twice each. Then she said something in a dialect Adam did not have, and Wren answered it, and whatever it was, it was the right thing, because the old woman's face changed.

And then she stopped, the way the animal had stopped, between one and the next.

Adam Rooke looked across the cot at the top of Wren's grey head, and at Sabet Orne's face, which had gone slack in the way faces do, and at the sheet of colourless nothing rising off a woman who had taught him the letter that made the sound *r* and had laughed at him for holding the pen like a knife.

He had four.

"Adam," said Wren, without looking up. "Go and get the cloth from the press. Bottom shelf, the coarse ones."

The residue was thinning. He could see the beam of the ceiling through it now where he had not been able to a moment ago.

"Yes," he said.

And afterwards — for a very long time afterwards, on other worlds, with a number in his head that had eleven digits in it — Adam Rooke would not be able to say with certainty which of the two questions in that room he had been answering.
