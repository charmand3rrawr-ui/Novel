# Chapter 4 — The Coarse Ones, Bottom Shelf

He got the cloths from the press, bottom shelf, the coarse ones, and he brought them back, and he helped Wren Halloway wash and wrap a body.

It took forty minutes and neither of them said anything for the first thirty.

She showed him how without appearing to show him: turned the arm so he could see how she wanted it turned, waited while he did the same on his side, adjusted his hands once with two fingers and no comment. He had never done this before and had never been near a body that was not on a television, and he found that the thing he had braced for — revulsion, or panic, or some large emotional weather — simply did not arrive.

What arrived instead was the *cold*. She had been warm on Tuesday when she took the chalk out of his hand. She was not warm now, and the not-warm was not like an object; an object has never been warm, and this was different, this was a thing that had been switched off, and the switching off was legible in her the way a stopped clock is legible.

He worked, and he thought about the residue.

It had thinned out and gone while he was in the corridor fetching cloths. He had known it would. He had known it while he was walking, at an ordinary pace, down a corridor he could have run down in nine seconds.

He turned that over while his hands folded coarse cloth over a woman's feet, and he arrived, with the grim punctuality that was the defining feature of his mind, at the thing he had been avoiding for forty minutes.

*I didn't refuse.*

*I walked. I walked at a normal speed to get cloths from a press, and in walking I let nine units thin out into a ward. And I have spent the whole of the last forty minutes allowing myself to believe that was a decision.*

He tucked the cloth under and squared it.

*It wasn't a decision. A decision has a moment in it. That was a man finding a way to have it both ways — to let it go, and to not have said no.*

"Adam."

He looked up. Wren was watching him across the cot.

"You've done that corner four times," she said.

He looked down. He had.

"Sorry."

"Don't apologise to me, apologise to her, and she won't mind either." Wren straightened up and pressed both hands into the small of her back. "You want to know what you're feeling."

"I don't think I'm feeling anything. That's rather the problem."

"Mm." She picked up the bowl. "You will. It doesn't come when they die. It comes about nine days later when you go to tell them something."

---

He did not sleep. At half past four he went out to the yard and sat on the step by the standpipe in the cold with his back against the wall.

The two moons were both up. The small white one had crossed a quarter of the sky since he had first seen it, which meant he could now estimate its period, and he did, roughly, and got something between nineteen and twenty-three hours, and felt the small clean pleasure of a number obtained.

Then he made himself do the other sum.

He had been on this world for six days and he had been offered two sources. One beast and one woman. He had taken one and let one go.

If the rate held — and six days was nothing, six days was noise, he would have laughed at anyone who quoted him a rate off six days — but if it held, this building would offer him something on the order of eight to ten sources a month.

At nine units each.

Eighty to ninety units a month. Against a baseline of three, earned in a field, that had made his forearms feel like a man's again.

He sat on a cold step and let himself see the whole shape of it for the first time, and the shape was not a plan, and it was not a temptation. It was simply a fact sitting in the yard with him, the way the standpipe was sitting in the yard, and it had been there since the bench on the first night.

*I have landed in the one building on this planet where my particular disease is also a career.*

He put his head back against the wall.

"No," he said.

And then, because he had never been able to leave a thing crooked, and because there was nobody in the yard to hear it:

"Not yet."

That was the second time he had said it. He noticed that it had come out easier than the first.

---

Wren found him in the yard at six and did not ask why he was there.

She sat down on the step beside him, which took her a while, and handed him a tin cup of something hot and bitter that was not tea and not coffee and did the job of both.

"Sabet had two sons," she said.

"South."

"One's in the ground at Corrow eleven years and she knew it, and the other's alive and hasn't written since the spring before that." Wren drank. "She said both names. She always said both names. I've watched her do it four times to four different people."

Adam held the cup in both hands.

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because you're going to spend today deciding she died peacefully, and she didn't, she died with an account she couldn't settle, and most of them do. You're new. I don't let new ones build a nice story in the first month. They break worse later."

He turned the cup a quarter-turn on his knee.

"I'd like to learn," he said.

"Learn what?"

"Whatever you're willing to teach and whatever Varn is willing to let me watch. I'd like to be useful in the room rather than in the corridor." He kept his voice level. "I carry water well. I'd rather do something that scales."

Wren Halloway looked at him sideways for a long moment.

"You said a strange thing there."

"Which part?"

"'Scales.'" She said the word the way you hold up something found in a pocket. "That's not a ward word. That's a word men use in offices."

Adam Rooke sat very still and understood, with a cold clarity that did not show on his face at all, that he had just made the first real mistake of his second life.

"I don't know where I got it," he said.

Which was, technically, true.

---

Wren did not raise the word again for three days, which was, he came to understand, how she worked. She did not interrogate. She let a thing sit, and then she put it down in front of you when your hands were full.

His hands were full of sheets when she did it.

"Where's Corrow," she said.

"I don't know."

"Sabet said it in front of you on the last night. You didn't ask." She took the other end of the sheet without being asked and they folded it between them, in and in, the way he had watched her do it forty times and had learned by watching. "A man who doesn't know where he is asks where everything is. You've been here eleven days and you've asked me the word for four objects and nothing else."

"I've been asking other people."

"You've been asking Petren, who is nine, and Dala, who will tell you anything, and you haven't asked me or Varn or anyone who'd wonder why you're asking." She squared the sheet on the pile. "You're doing it carefully. That's what I'm telling you. I'm not telling you off. I'm telling you I can see the shape of it."

He stood with his hands empty.

"I don't know what I am," he said.

And that was true, and it came out of him with a rawness that surprised them both.

"No," said Wren. "I don't think you do."

She picked up the pile.

"Here's what I've decided, and you can have it straight, because I'm sixty-one and I've stopped enjoying the long way round." She settled the sheets on her hip. "I've had four like you through this house. Men who turn up with nothing and no story and are better at things than they ought to be. Two were running from something they'd done. One had been beaten so badly he'd lost eleven years and it came back in the spring and he wept for a week. And one was exactly what he said he was, and I was cruel to him for a month for no reason at all, and he left, and I think about it."

"Which am I?"

"I don't know yet, and I'm not going to guess, because guessing is how I got the fourth one wrong." She looked at him levelly. "So you'll get what everyone gets. A mattress, three meals, and nobody asking. And in exchange you'll do the work, and you'll not lie to me in a way that costs somebody in those beds."

"That seems fair."

"It isn't fair, it's just the arrangement." She started to go, and stopped. "One more thing, and then I'll not say it again."

"Go on."

"You were four minutes getting cloths from a press that's eleven steps from cot nine."

The corridor was very quiet.

"I counted," said Wren Halloway. "I count everything, it's the only thing I've got. I'm not accusing you of anything. There's nothing in that press worth stealing and you'd have had the sense not to. But you were four minutes, and when you came back your face had something on it I didn't like, and I've thought about it twice a day since."

She waited.

Adam Rooke stood in a corridor and found that he had prepared for eleven versions of this conversation and none of them was this one, because none of them had involved being seen by someone who was not hostile.

"I stopped in the corridor," he said. "I couldn't go back in for a minute."

"Why?"

"Because she taught me eleven letters on Tuesday," he said, "and I hadn't thanked her, and I was standing in a corridor working out that there was no longer any arrangement of events in which I could."

Wren looked at him for a long moment.

"That one I believe," she said, and went.

He stood where he was for some time after she had gone, and what he felt was not guilt, and it was not relief.

It was the recognition — cold, precise, filed immediately — that he had just discovered how well a true thing works when it is placed next to an absence.

He had not lied. He had simply told her the second reason.

*That,* thought Adam, *is going to be extremely useful,* and then stood very still, because he had not meant to think it.

---

She let him into the theatre on the following Thursday, and she did it in the worst possible way, which was without warning.

"Rooke. In here."

He put down the mop.

Ilse Varn had a man on the table and the man was conscious and the man's left hand was in a condition Adam did not have a category for. Something industrial. Something with rollers. The apprentices were both there and one of them had gone the colour of the wall.

"Hold that," said Varn.

"Hold what?"

"That." She indicated with her chin. "Both hands. Firm. If you faint, faint backwards, I don't want you on the field."

He held it.

It was warm and slick and it moved, and the man on the table made a sound, and Adam Rooke's entire nervous system attempted to leave through the soles of his feet, and he held on.

And then, about ninety seconds in, something happened that he did not expect and could not afterwards entirely explain.

He stopped being frightened, and started *reading*.

The hand was a mechanism. That was all it was. It was a mechanism that had been disassembled by an external force, and he was looking at the disassembly, and the disassembly had a structure — this had gone first, and that had torn because this had gone, and that flap of tissue was not destroyed, it had been *displaced*, and it had come from there, and it would go back.

He had spent sixteen years looking at four hundred pages of something and finding the three assumptions that were load-bearing.

Later he would try to describe it to himself properly, because he distrusted anything he could not describe, and the best he ever managed was this: it was like the moment when a page of figures stops being a page of figures.

He had felt it perhaps nine times in sixteen years. You look at a schedule of claims and for a while it is just rows, and then — without effort, without any sense of having done anything — it is not rows any more, it is a *situation*, and you can see the shape of it, and the shape has a fault in it, and the fault is over there.

The hand went from meat to mechanism between one breath and the next.

The man on the table was called Teor and he was a millwright and he was twenty-six, and the roller had taken him at the second knuckle and dragged. Adam registered all of that and set it aside, not because he was callous but because it was not load-bearing for the next four minutes, and he had always been able to do that with a speed that had frightened at least two of the three therapists.

What was load-bearing:

The outer two digits were not attached to anything that mattered. He could see that from where he stood, because the tissue on the dorsal side had gone entirely and what remained was moving when Varn moved it, which meant it was moving *passively*, which meant nothing was holding it.

The middle one was different. When Varn's probe touched the base of it, the whole digit did something small and coordinated, and coordinated means attached, and attached means alive.

The bleeding at the top was arterial and dramatic and was going to stop by itself or with four minutes of work. The bleeding at the base was slow, dark, and continuous, and it had already filled the gutter of the drape twice while everyone looked at the top.

And on the tray, in among the debris an apprentice had cleared away, there was a piece of tissue about the size of a thumbnail, roughly oval, and it had a whorl on it.

Skin with a print on it does not come from nowhere. It comes from the pad of a finger, and there was only one pad missing, and it was the one on the digit that was still alive.

"You're not squeamish," said Varn, without looking up.

"I appear not to be."

"Hm." She worked for a while. "Then don't stand there. Tell me what you see."

He thought it was a trap, or a test, or contempt. He said it anyway, because the alternative was saying nothing and being a man who carries water.

"The two on the outside are gone and you're not going to get them," he said. "The middle one's attached at the back and it's the only one with a chance, so everything you do should be about that one, and the bleeding at the base is worse than the bleeding at the top even though the top looks worse. And there's a piece of him on the tray that isn't rubbish."

The room went quiet.

Varn stopped, with her hands still inside the man's hand, and looked up at Adam for the first time in six days.

"Say the last one again."

"On the tray. The second thing your apprentice put there. It's not debris, it's the pad off the middle finger, and it's the whole pad, and it's got the skin on it."

Ilse Varn turned her head and looked at the tray.

Then she said a word Adam did not know, and the apprentice who had gone the colour of the wall went and got the tray, and Varn worked for another forty minutes and did not speak to anybody, and at the end of it the man on the table had three fingers instead of two.

---

She caught him in the corridor afterwards, with her sleeves still rolled.

"Where did you train?"

"I didn't."

"Don't."

"I'm not being clever with you." He put the mop against the wall, deliberately, so that his hands were empty and visible. "I don't have any training. I don't have any memory of training. I woke up in a field east of here nine days ago and I can't read a ward board."

Varn folded her arms.

She was perhaps forty-five, and up close she had the particular exhaustion of someone who has been the only competent person in eleven consecutive rooms, and her left hand — the one she laid on sternums — had a tremor in it at rest that was not present when she worked.

"You saw the pad," she said.

"It was on the tray."

"Three people were looking at that tray, and two of them have four years of training, and none of them saw it." She studied him. "That isn't a talent. That's not how talent presents. Talent presents as fast hands or a good ear or a memory. What you did was *notice*, and noticing is the one thing I have never once been able to teach."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a problem." She unrolled one sleeve. "Because I come here on Thursdays, and I have nine minutes per patient, and I have two apprentices who are good and will be adequate, and I will be dead or shaking too badly to hold a needle in eleven years, and this house will get whatever the Accord sends it, which will be nobody."

She unrolled the other sleeve.

"Can you count?" she said.

"Yes."

"How well?"

Adam Rooke looked at a tired surgeon in a corridor and made a decision he would revisit for the rest of his life.

"Better than anyone you have ever met," he said.

---

She tested him for two hours that evening, and she was not gentle about it.

She started with the drug book, which he could not read, so she read it to him — dosages by weight, in a system of units he had to build from scratch while she was talking — and then asked him for a dose for a patient at a weight she gave him in the middle of a sentence about something else.

He gave it to her.

She changed the weight. He changed the dose.

She gave him a weight in one unit and a concentration in another and a volume in a third, and he asked her, mildly, whether the third was the one that had twelve in it or the one that had sixteen, and Ilse Varn stopped and put her pen down.

"Nobody asks that," she said.

"Everybody should ask that."

"Everybody *should*." She picked the pen up again. "Right. The one with sixteen."

He gave her the answer in about four seconds and she checked it on paper and it took her ninety and she got the same number.

Then she gave him the mortality tables.

He did not know they were mortality tables until she was three rows in, and then he did, and something in his chest went sideways and he had to look at the wall for a second.

"Problem?"

"No."

"You went somewhere."

"I've seen a table like that before," said Adam, and it was the truest thing he had said on this world except one, and he said it to a surgeon in a store room at nine at night with a lamp between them, and she did not know what she was being told.

He read it — she read it, he listened — and at the end of it he said:

"Your survivals are wrong."

"They're not mine. They're the Accord's."

"Then the Accord's are wrong, and they're wrong in a specific way." He held his hand out for the paper he could not read, which was a foolish thing to do, and she put it in his hand anyway. "You've got a column that drops off at the fourth day and stays flat, and that's not a survival curve, that's two populations. Somebody's put people who were going to live and people who were going to die into the same table, and the shape of it is the shape of a number that was made by adding."

Ilse Varn sat back on the stool.

"How can you possibly know that from hearing it read aloud."

"Because things that come from one cause have one shape," said Adam, "and that's got two, and there's a knee in it at the fourth day where nothing in the body changes on the fourth day." He put the paper down. "Something changes in the *admission* on the fourth day. Do you discharge on the fourth day?"

"We transfer on the fourth day," said Varn slowly. "If they're stable at four days, they go to Seventh. Seventh has the beds."

"Then the table isn't measuring survival. It's measuring survival *here*, and everybody who's doing well leaves on day four and gets counted as an outcome you never see. Your survivals aren't wrong because your medicine is bad. They're wrong because you're being measured on the ones you keep."

The lamp hissed.

"Eleven years," said Ilse Varn. "Eleven years I have been going to the Accord with those figures and asking for a second surgeon, and eleven years they have told me my outcomes don't justify it."

"They don't. That's the point. They can't. The way you're counted, they never will."

She sat and looked at him.

"What were you?" she said. "Before the field."

And Adam Rooke, who had promised himself on a road at four in the morning that he would say nothing to anybody about anything, heard himself say:

"I was a man who found the assumption underneath the number."

---

It happened on the ninth day, exactly as she had said it would, and he had the grace to find that infuriating.

He had got the growling letter into a word.

Not just the letter — the letter inside a word, in the middle, where it was hardest, because Sabet had warned him the middle was where it hid. He had been working at the ward board for three evenings with a stub of chalk and Petren correcting him with the pitiless accuracy of a nine-year-old, and on the ninth evening he looked at the second line of the board and the shapes came apart into sounds and the sounds came together into a word he knew.

It was the word for *water*. It was not a magnificent word. It was on a board in a charity hospital and it referred to buckets.

Adam Rooke stood in front of a slate with a stub of chalk in his hand and read a word on another world, and he turned round to tell her.

He got two steps.

Cot nine had a man in it called Brenner who had been there five days with something wrong in his gut, and Brenner was asleep, and the blanket was folded at the foot in the way Wren folded them, and there was no reason on earth for the cot to look any different than it looked.

Adam stood in the middle of the long room with the chalk in his hand.

It was not grief. He wanted to be accurate about this even at the time, and he was, and being accurate about it did not help in the slightest. Grief he had some notion of; he had buried a father he had not spoken to in twenty-four years and had felt a sort of administrative sadness that had lasted about nine days.

This was smaller and it was worse. It was the specific experience of having something ready to hand over and nowhere to put it down.

He had a word. He had gone and got it. He had done the work she set him, nine times each and then in a different order, and he had got the hard one out of the middle of a word on the third evening, and there was no arrangement of events in which he could give it to her.

He stood there long enough that Petren asked him if he was ill.

"No," said Adam. "I've read a word."

"Which one?"

He showed him.

"That's *water*," said Petren, with enormous scorn. "Everyone knows that one."

"Yes," said Adam. "So I understand."

He went out into the yard and sat down on the step by the standpipe and put the heels of his hands into his eyes and stayed like that for a while, and it was the first time since the kitchen floor that anything had got all the way through, and it had been let in by a nine-year-old being rude about a noun.

*Nine days,* he thought, when he could. *She said nine days and it was nine days.*

*She's been doing this for forty years and she can price it to the day, and nobody has ever written it down, and if somebody had written it down there would be a table, and if there were a table you could plan for it, and nobody would have to find out the way I just did.*

He took his hands away from his face and looked at the standpipe, and the sky was going grey at the edge, and somewhere behind him the house was starting to wake up.

It occurred to him — and this was the thing that stayed, the thing he would still have in him on a dead world a long time later — that he had just had the only honest reaction of his second life, and that his response to having it had been, within ninety seconds, to start designing a table.

---

He lay on the mattress in the store room that night and did not sleep, and at some point in the small hours the words came up unbidden for the fifth time in his life.

**COMPREHENSION +1**

He looked at it in the dark.

He had absorbed nothing. Nobody had died within a hundred yards of him for three days. He had carried no water since morning. He had sat in a store room for two hours and argued with a surgeon about the shape of a curve, and the thing in his head had watched him do it and had decided that something in him had got larger.

*So it isn't only the dying.*

He lay very still.

*It reads what I do. It rewards what I do. It gave me endurance for carrying and it gave me this for* — 

He stopped, because the honest end of the sentence was *for being what I already was*, and because the implication of that was not a comfort.

It meant the thing in his head had a view about him.

It meant that somewhere behind **ASSESSMENT PENDING** there was a criterion, and the criterion had looked at a man taking a mortality table apart in a store room and had approved.

He turned onto his side and looked at the dark shape of the shelving.

*And a criterion implies an assessor.*

He had been avoiding that for eleven days, the way he had avoided the obvious conclusion in the field, and for the same reason: it was not yet load-bearing. He could function without settling it. But it had been getting heavier, and lying in the dark with a **+1** still fading behind his eyes he made himself pick it up and feel the weight of it once, properly, before putting it down again.

Somebody had built this. Or something had. Or it had grown, the way a tumour grows, with a logic but no author.

Whichever it was, the language was the tell, and the language had been bothering him since the field. *Assessment. Unregistered. Projected yield.* Those were not the words of a gift. They were not the words of a god or a curse or a blessing. They were the words of a *scheme* — of an instrument with terms and conditions, written by somebody, for somebody's benefit, and he had spent sixteen years reading documents in exactly that register and he knew what he knew about them.

He knew that nobody writes *projected* unless there is a party who will be disappointed.

He knew that nobody writes *unregistered* unless registration exists, and is administered, and confers something.

And he knew — this above all, this was the thing he had told eleven hundred people across sixteen years in eleven different rooms — that in any document of this kind, the party who drafted it is not the party carrying the risk.

*So who is the beneficiary,* thought Adam Rooke, in a store room, on a world with two moons, *and what does the policy pay out on?*

He did not have anything approaching an answer. He had four data points and no control, and he would have laughed at anyone who quoted him a view.

But he noticed, as he finally went under, that he had stopped thinking of the thing in his head as a power.

He had started thinking of it as a *contract*, and he had started looking for the clause.

And somewhere behind that, further down, in the place the actuarial voice did not reach: he thought about Sabet Orne's residue thinning out into the rafters while he walked to a press at an ordinary speed, and he wondered — for the first time, and not for the last — whether that had also been scored.

And whether it had been scored as a refusal, or as a waste.
