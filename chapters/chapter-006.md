# Chapter 6 — Forty Crowns

The pump cost forty crowns and Mercy House Ninth had eleven.

Adam established this on the twenty-fourth day, and establishing it took him four hours, and the four hours were not spent on arithmetic. They were spent getting Wren Halloway to let him see the accounts, which she did not want to do, and which she eventually did for a reason that surprised him.

"You'll see what we spend on the dead," she said, putting the box on the intake bench. "Everyone does it in a different order and everyone gets to that line and stops. I'd rather you got there while I'm standing here."

He got there in the second column of the second page.

*Winding cloth, coarse — 2 crowns, 4.*

Per month. Against a total monthly outgoing of thirty-one crowns and some change.

He looked at it for a while.

"You bury them," he said.

"We wrap them and the parish takes them. The parish charges nothing and never has, which is the only piece of grace in the whole document." Wren's arms were folded. "The cloth we pay for. I've had two people tell me to use sacking."

"What did you say?"

"I said no, and then I said it again in a way they remembered."

Adam turned the page.

He did not say what he was thinking, which was that two crowns four a month was eight per cent of the house's entire operating cost, spent on a material whose only function was to be seen once by nobody. He did not say it because he had learned — in sixteen years, in eleven different rooms — that the cheapest way to lose a room is to price something they have already decided is not for sale.

And because he had looked at the line and found that he agreed with her, which was interesting, and which he set aside to examine later.

---

The accounts were, once he had them, the most informative object he had encountered on this world.

He could read perhaps one word in three now, and the numerals cleanly, and Petren had been promoted from boiled-sweet informant to salaried reader at a rate of one sweet per page, which was ruinous and which Adam paid without complaint.

By the fourth hour he had the shape.

Mercy House Ninth received an allocation from the Ninefold Accord of twenty-two crowns a month. It received, from a standing endowment laid down two hundred and ten years ago by a woman whose name Petren could not pronounce, a further six crowns and two. It received, in charitable gifts, an amount that varied between nothing and four crowns and averaged one and a half.

Twenty-nine and a half, against thirty-one and change.

They were running at a loss of about two crowns a month and had been for eleven years, and the loss had been absorbed, entirely, by Wren Halloway not being paid in seven of those years.

He found that in a marginal note. It was not flagged, not summarised, not in any total. It was four words in the left margin of a page from three years ago, in her handwriting, and Petren read it out without any idea what he was reading.

*Halloway — deferred — carry.*

Adam sat in the intake room with a stub of chalk and did not move for some time.

*Deferred. Not waived. She's been writing it down. She's been recording a debt the house owes her that the house will never pay, for seven years, in her own hand, in the margin, so that it's true.*

He thought about a woman who counted everything because it was the only thing she had.

Then he did something he was not proud of afterwards, and which he did without hesitating: he memorised the figure.

*Forty-one crowns, eight. That's what the house owes her. That is the price of Wren Halloway, and I now know it, and she does not know I know it.*

He put the page back exactly as he had found it, squared to the edge of the box, and then — deliberately — nudged it crooked.

It did not help. He sat looking at the crooked page for a while and found that the gesture had become what gestures become when you know why you make them, which is nothing at all.

*Four weeks,* he thought. *Four weeks, and I have started keeping a second ledger in my head, and everyone in this building is in it.*

*Wren: forty-one crowns eight, and a thing she will not use sacking for.*
*Varn: a tremor in the left hand and eleven years of hearings.*
*Kassick: four years of a measure he changed and never mentioned.*
*Petren: a hole in his heart and a rate of one sweet a page.*

He did not write any of it down. He had never needed to write anything down.

---

The endowment was the interesting part, and it was interesting in a way that made him briefly, genuinely happy, for the first time since the kitchen.

"Six crowns and two," he said. "Every month. Since when?"

"Since it was laid down. Two hundred and ten years."

"The same six and two."

"The same six and two." Wren had brought him a cup of the hot bitter stuff and had sat down on the other end of the bench, which she had started doing in the evenings and which neither of them had commented on. "It's a fixed sum. That's what a fixed sum is."

"Do you know what it bought when it was laid down?"

She looked at him.

"No," she said slowly. "No, I don't suppose I've ever asked that."

"Nobody ever does," said Adam. "That's what I did for a living. I'm almost certain that's what I did for a living. People come to you with a number that was set a long time ago and they've been arguing about whether it's being paid properly, and nobody in the room has asked what it was *for*."

He set the cup down.

"Six crowns and two, two hundred and ten years ago, in a document that also specifies the number of beds. How many beds?"

Wren went very still.

"Forty-one," she said.

"Forty-one beds. And six crowns and two." He leaned forward. "Wren. That woman did not leave you six crowns and two. She left you *the cost of running forty-one beds*, and she wrote the number down because in her century writing the number down was the same thing as writing the intention down. They hadn't invented the idea that money could stop meaning what it meant."

The intake room was quiet. Somewhere down the ward a man was coughing in the long, patient way of someone who has been doing it for weeks.

"It's not enough for four beds now," said Wren.

"It's not enough for two."

"And you're going to tell me there's something to be done about it."

"I have no idea," said Adam. "I don't know this world's law. I don't know if an endowment can be restated, or who would hear it, or whether the woman's family still exists and would fight it. There might be nothing. I want to be honest about that, because I've watched people be given hope on a document and it is a filthy thing to do."

He picked the cup back up.

"But somebody should find out," he said. "And nobody has, for two hundred and ten years, because everybody who has ever read that line has read it as an amount instead of an intention."

---

He took the endowment to Varn on the Thursday, in the nine minutes she had between the third patient and the fourth, and she heard him out with her hands in a bowl and her back to him.

"Restatement," she said.

"Is that a word here?"

"It's a word." She dried her hands. "It's a word that means eleven years in a chancery and four advocates and a bond against costs that this house could not raise if it sold the roof."

"How much is the bond?"

"Two hundred crowns."

Adam did the sum without meaning to. *Six years, eight months of the total endowment. Or Wren's deferred wages, five times over.*

"Then it isn't a route," he said.

"It isn't a route." Varn leaned against the table. She looked worse than she had a fortnight ago; there was a grey underneath the tiredness now, and the tremor in the left hand had been visible twice that morning when she was not using it. "That's the first thing you've brought me that doesn't work, and I want you to notice that I'm pleased about it."

"Why?"

"Because a man who only ever brings me things that work is a man who's selling." She said it without unkindness. "Bring me four more that don't and I'll start believing you."

He filed that, and did not enjoy how quickly he filed it.

"There's a second question," he said. "It's smaller, and I think it's better."

"Go on."

"The endowment specifies forty-one beds. You have forty-one beds. Has anybody ever tested whether the Accord's allocation is *conditional* on the endowment's terms being met — or whether the endowment's terms are conditional on the Accord's allocation?"

Varn frowned. "What's the difference?"

"The difference is about twenty-two crowns a month." He took a breath and slowed himself down, because he could hear that he had started talking at the speed he used to talk at in rooms where he was the only person who could see the thing. "Suppose the Accord cuts you to eighteen. You can't staff forty-one beds on eighteen. So you close nine, and you're a thirty-two bed house. Fine. Except the endowment says forty-one. If you run thirty-two beds, are you still complying with a two-hundred-and-ten-year-old instrument that pays you six and two a month?"

There was a pause.

"I don't know," said Ilse Varn.

"Neither do I. But the shape of it is this: if the endowment *lapses* when you drop below forty-one beds, then the Accord's cut doesn't cost you twenty-two crowns. It costs you twenty-eight and two, and the last six and two are permanent and cannot be got back, ever, and the person in the capital who signs the cut has no idea."

He put both hands flat on the table.

"And if that's true," he said, "then it is not a plea. It is a *warning*, and warnings get read by a different department."

Varn stood very still.

"You have been in this house four weeks," she said.

"Twenty-nine days."

"Twenty-nine days, and you have found the pump, the ratio, a supplier, and now this." She picked up her case. "I want to say something and I want it understood as a compliment and a caution in one, and I'm not going to say it twice because the fourth patient has a hernia and no patience."

"All right."

"You are the most useful person who has ever walked into this building," said Ilse Varn, "and every single thing you have found, you found by looking at how a thing is *counted* rather than what it is. The pump was a column width. The ratio was a denominator. The endowment is a definition."

She stopped at the door.

"One day something in this house is going to be a person and not a number," she said, "and I would very much like to be standing somewhere I can watch what you do."

---

The pump, in the end, was not solved by any of that.

The pump was solved on the twenty-ninth day by a man called Bern Kassick, who supplied the house with coal, oil and lamp-wick, and who had been overcharging Mercy House Ninth for four years.

Adam found it because he could not read.

That was the honest account of it, and he gave the honest account to Varn later, and she did not believe him, and he could not make her.

He could not read Kassick's invoices, so he did to them what he had done to the ledgers: he measured them. Nine invoices, one a quarter, laid out on the bench in order. Same ruling, same hand, same columns. He was looking for the fourth column because the fourth column was where numbers lived, and his eye — trained in three weeks on a world with a script he could not read — had become very good indeed at the geometry of a page.

And the ninth invoice had a different geometry.

Not the numbers. The *column widths*. Somewhere between the sixth invoice and the seventh, the ruled line separating the quantity from the price had moved about a sixteenth of an inch to the left.

He sat and looked at it for a long time.

Then he got Petren, and paid him four sweets, and had him read out every quantity and every price on all nine.

The quantities were consistent. The prices were consistent. The *units* had changed. Somewhere between the sixth invoice and the seventh, Kassick had stopped billing by the long measure and started billing by the short one, and the price per unit had stayed exactly the same, and the column had moved because the word in it was one character shorter.

It was, Adam thought, rather elegant. He was aware that admiring it was a symptom of something.

"How much?" said Wren, when he told her.

"Over four years? Thirty-one crowns and change." He paused. "Which is, as it happens, almost exactly a pump."

---

Kassick came on the first of the month and Adam met him in the yard.

He had thought about this for two days. He had thought about it the way he used to think about a difficult meeting — not about what he would say, which is what amateurs prepare, but about what the other man needed to be able to do at the end of it.

Bern Kassick was about fifty, heavy through the shoulders, with the specific weathered competence of a man who has moved goods for thirty years. He was not a monster. He was, almost certainly, a man who had switched to the short measure with every customer he had, because the short measure was legal and everyone else had done it, and who had simply never mentioned it to the charity house because the charity house had never asked.

"You're the new one," said Kassick.

"Adam."

"Halloway said you'd want a word."

"I do. It's four minutes and then you can get on." Adam had the nine invoices under his arm and he did not produce them. That was deliberate too. "I want to tell you what I've found, and then I want to tell you what I'm going to do about it, and I'd like you to hear the second part before you decide about the first."

Kassick's face closed a little. "Go on."

"Four years ago you changed from the long measure to the short. Your price per unit didn't move. The house has paid thirty-one crowns and four more than it would have."

The yard was quiet. Kassick stood with his thumbs in his belt.

"That's legal," he said.

"Entirely. I've checked as far as I can check, and I can't read, so I had a nine-year-old check it too, and he agrees with you." Adam let that sit. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I want that to be very clear, because I think in about ten seconds you're going to feel accused, and I'd rather you didn't, because it'll make this take longer."

"Then what do you want?"

"Here's the part I want you to hear first," said Adam. "I am not going to take this to the Accord. I'm not going to take it to the parish. I'm not going to tell anyone in this house except the two who already know, and neither of them will say a word, because one of them is sixty-one and discreet and the other is nine and can be bought with sweets."

Kassick looked at him.

"Why not?"

"Because you have supplied this house for eleven years, and in the winter three years ago you delivered coal in a week when two other houses got none, and Halloway remembers it, and I have read the ledger and it is true." Adam shifted the invoices under his arm. "You are not a man who cheats a charity. You are a man who changed a measure in his book and never once looked at which of his customers couldn't check."

Bern Kassick stood in the yard of Mercy House Ninth for about nine seconds without saying anything.

"There's four other houses on my round," he said at last. "Charitable ones."

"I know. I don't want their money either and it isn't mine to want."

"Then what do you *want*, man?"

Adam had thought about this part for a long time and had arrived, in the end, at a principle he had used perhaps four times in sixteen years and had never once explained to anybody.

*Never ask a man for what he owes. Ask him for slightly more than he owes, in a different currency, and let him feel the difference is a decision he made.*

A man asked for thirty-one crowns and four has been *caught*, and will pay it, and will hate the house for the rest of his life, and will find the eleven small ways a supplier can punish a customer without ever being in breach of anything.

A man asked for forty crowns as a pump has been *offered a transaction*, and can tell his wife about it at dinner, and will deliver on the fourteenth rather than the twenty-ninth.

"Forty crowns," said Adam. "Not thirty-one and four — forty. Delivered as a pump, installed, by the end of the month. In exchange the house will sign a supply agreement with you for six years at the short measure, priced fairly, and I will write the agreement myself, and it will be the best document you have ever signed, because you will not have to think about your measure again for six years and neither will anybody else."

"That's more than I took."

"It's eight crowns and six more than you took. You're buying two things with the difference." Adam held up one finger. "You're buying silence, which you could probably have had for free, and which I'd have given you anyway, and I'm telling you that because I want you to know what the price actually covers." He held up the second. "And you're buying six years of a customer who has just proved she can read your invoices."

Kassick started to laugh. It came out of him unwillingly.

"You can't read at all."

"Not a word of it," said Adam. "I measured the columns."

---

The pump went in on the fourteenth of the following month, and it took two men a day and a half, and when they opened the tap in the long room the water came out brown for four minutes and then ran clear.

Wren Halloway stood in the middle of the ward with a bucket she was not going to need and did not say anything for rather a long time.

"Eleven years," she said.

"Eleven years and a supply agreement."

"You've cost him eight crowns."

"I've cost him eight crowns and saved him about four hundred, because in six years somebody in one of those other houses is going to notice the measure and it is going to be very much worse than a conversation in a yard." Adam turned the tap off. "He knows that. That's why he signed. He signed fast and he read it twice, which is the behaviour of a man who has been waiting to be caught."

"You didn't catch him."

"No," said Adam. "I sold him a way of having been caught quietly. They're different products and the second one is worth more."

Wren looked at him sideways.

"That," she said, "is the most unpleasant sentence anyone has ever said in this ward, and I have heard a man describe what he'd done to his brother."

"It's accurate."

"I didn't say it wasn't accurate." Wren filled the bucket she was not going to need, and emptied it, and filled it again, apparently for the pleasure of it. "I said it was unpleasant. Those aren't opposites. I've spent forty years watching people be one and not the other and I'd have said the accurate ones were better company, and now I'm less sure."

"Would you rather I hadn't done it?"

"No." She set the bucket down. "That's the trouble. I've been trying for an hour to find the version where you were kind to him instead, and every version I build ends with no pump."

She looked at the tap.

"There's a thing that happens to people who run houses like this," she said. "You start out refusing to be clever, because clever is how the world takes things off the poor. And then you watch four winters. And by the fifth you'd sell your own name for a working boiler and you tell yourself the difference is that *you'd* only use it for good."

"And?"

"And nobody ever does," said Wren Halloway. "That's all. I'm not warning you. I'm too tired and it's too late for you. I'm just telling you I've seen the road and it's well made and it goes somewhere."

---

Petren asked him about the sweets on the eleventh evening and Adam gave him the true answer, which was a mistake, and which he did not regret.

"Why d'you pay me?"

"Because you're doing work."

"Dala reads for Wren and she doesn't get anything."

"Dala is thirty-four and paid a wage. You're nine and you're not." Adam turned a page. "Also because if I don't pay you, you'll do it out of goodwill, and goodwill runs out and a rate doesn't."

Petren considered this with the gravity of a small boy who has been addressed as a contracting party.

"That's mean," he decided.

"It's extremely mean. It's also why you'll still be doing it in four weeks and you'd have stopped in two."

The boy had a hole in his heart. Adam had established that on the second day and had been unable to stop knowing it since. It was not a metaphor and it was not curable in this house; it was a defect that Varn had described, in the flat shorthand she used for things she could not fix, as *the gap*, and it meant Petren could not run more than about forty yards and went blue at the lips in cold weather and would probably not see twenty.

He was, in Adam's private and unspoken accounting, the single most reliable source in the building.

"What's this one?" Petren said, pointing.

"That's *held*."

"You know that one."

"I know that one because you taught it to me and I've been saying it in my head for three weeks like a madman." Adam turned the ledger round. "Here. Your turn. Read me the whole line."

Petren read him the whole line. It took him a while and he got two words wrong and Adam did not correct either of them, because the boy had corrected himself on the third attempt both times and the correcting was the part that mattered.

"You're better than me," said Adam.

"You're *grown*."

"Yes. It's very annoying."

Petren laughed, and then coughed, and the cough had the wrong shape in it, and Adam sat still and did not let anything show on his face at all and waited for it to pass.

Nothing. No words. No line of pale colour along the ground.

He had not expected any. The boy was not within an hour of anything. But he had noticed that he had *checked*, and the noticing arrived the way a stone arrives in a shoe, and he sat on a bench in an intake room with a nine-year-old beside him and looked at the far wall for a while.

"Adam."

"Mm."

"Are you going to stay?"

He turned his head.

It was asked in the deliberately careless way children ask the questions they have rehearsed, looking at the page rather than at him, one heel knocking against the bench leg.

"I don't know," said Adam.

"Dala says you'll go. She says the clever ones always go."

"Dala's probably right. She's right about most things and she's been here nineteen years."

"Oh," said Petren.

And Adam Rooke, who had promised himself on a road at four in the morning that he would say nothing to anybody about anything, heard himself say:

"But I'd like to stay until you can read the whole board without me, and that's going to take you about four months, because you're lazy about the long ones."

"I'm *not* lazy—"

"You skipped two of them tonight and hoped I wouldn't notice."

"You *can't read*."

"I can read enough to count how many words are in a line," said Adam, "which is all I needed, and you have just told me which two."

Petren went red to the ears and then, grudgingly, grinned.

---

He learned to read on the sixteenth, properly, all at once, in the way that it happens.

There was no moment. That was what nobody had told him and what he found almost offensive: he had been expecting a threshold, a click, a line crossed, and instead he sat down on the step by the standpipe on the sixteenth evening with the ward board in front of him and realised he had been reading it for about four days.

He went and got the accounts and read a whole page without Petren.

Then he went and got the ledger for the current year and read the last month of it, every line, every cause of death, every day-count, and it took him an hour and eleven minutes, and when he had finished he sat with his hands flat on the bench and understood that he had just done in an hour what had taken him six hours and a nine-year-old three weeks earlier.

He read the words in the middle of his vision, and for the first time since the field he was actively angry at them.

"I did that," he said, out loud, in an empty intake room. "That was three weeks of work and a child's boiled sweets and sitting on a step every night. That was *mine*."

The words did not answer. They faded the way they always faded.

And Adam Rooke sat alone with an open ledger and understood, in the part of him that had always understood things slightly before he wanted to, that he had just made the first genuinely frightening discovery of his second life — which was not that the thing in his head could take.

It was that it could *claim*.

---

That night he wrote a letter he could not send.

He did it on the back of a spoiled intake sheet, in the store room, by the light of a lamp he was not supposed to be using, and he wrote it in a script he had learned from a dying woman, in a language that was not the language he had thought in for thirty-eight years.

It took him three hours and it was four lines long.

*Renne. I have found out what the assumption was. It was not that you loved me. I checked that one for eleven years and it held. It was that I thought being useful was the same as being wanted, and I priced everything off it, and you knew, and you used the right instrument.*

*I am not writing to forgive you. I am writing because there is nobody here I can say a true thing to.*

*I have taken something from a dying animal and let something go from a dying woman and I cannot yet tell you which of those frightens me.*

*The boiler here makes a different sound.*

He read it back once.

Then he held the corner of it in the lamp flame and watched it go, and put the ash out with his boot, and lay down on the mattress, and was asleep in four minutes, which had not happened since the kitchen.

In the morning Wren asked him why he looked better and he said he had slept, and she said "About time," and put him on the water for the third week running, and the pump ran clear, and nobody in the long room had to carry anything at all.

---

There was one more thing, and it happened on the last day of the month, and he did not understand it for another two years.

He was in the yard at dusk fitting a washer to the new tap — a job that needed doing, that nobody had asked him to do, and that he had taken on because his hands wanted something — when the pressure came at the back of his neck.

He knew what it was before he turned round. He had felt it once, on a road, at three in the morning, with a woman going past him at forty miles an hour.

There was a man standing at the gate of Mercy House Ninth.

He was not doing anything. He was perhaps sixty feet away, on the road, in an unremarkable coat, and he was looking at the building — not at Adam, at the *building*, the way a surveyor looks at a wall.

Adam stayed where he was with the washer in his fingers.

The man looked at the building for about eleven seconds. Then he took something from his coat, and wrote on it, and put it back. Then he turned and walked away up the road at an ordinary speed, and the pressure at the back of Adam's neck went with him, and by the time Adam had got to the gate the road was empty in both directions for a quarter of a mile.

He stood at the gate for a long time.

Then he went back and finished the washer, and tested the tap, and it did not drip.

And in the middle of his vision, unsummoned, for about two seconds, so briefly that he was never afterwards entirely certain he had seen it:

**FLAGGED.**
**OBSERVED.**
