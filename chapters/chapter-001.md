# Chapter 1 — Eleven Months

The mortality table said the man would live another thirty-one years, and Adam Rooke had been looking at it for nine minutes without touching his keyboard.

That was unusual. He was not a slow worker. He had built a reputation at Halloran & Vance on being the man who could look at four hundred pages of claims data and tell you, by the end of the afternoon, which three assumptions in it were load-bearing and which of those three was wrong. He did it without ceremony. He did it without making anyone feel stupid, which was rarer, and which was most of why they kept him.

But the table on his screen had a shape he did not like, and he had not worked out why yet.

It was a group life scheme. Eleven hundred employees, mid-sized logistics firm, nothing remarkable in the demographics. The premiums were priced off a standard table with a small adjustment for the fact that a hundred and forty of the eleven hundred drove articulated lorries for a living. That adjustment was the only interesting number on the page, and it was correct. He had checked it twice.

The shape he did not like was elsewhere. It was in the lapse rate.

People let policies go. That was normal; it was priced in. What was not normal was the way these particular people let them go — not smoothly, the way a population drifts away from a product over years, but in small tight clusters, three or four at a time, and then nothing for months, and then another cluster.

Clusters meant a cause. A cause meant the model was wrong, because the model assumed independence, and clustered lapses are the opposite of independent.

He wrote a note to himself in the margin of the file. *Lapse clustering — check restructures? Depot closures?* Then he wrote, underneath it, in the same neat hand: *Not urgent.*

It was, in fact, worth about four hundred thousand pounds to somebody, but it was not urgent, and he had learned a long time ago that the fastest way to be ignored was to arrive out of breath.

His phone buzzed face-down on the desk. He turned it over with one finger.

*Don't forget tonight. 8. I'm doing the thing with the lamb.*

He looked at it for a second longer than the message required.

*Wouldn't forget,* he typed. *Should I bring anything?*

*Just you.*

He set the phone down, screen to the desk, squared to the edge of the blotter. Then he moved it a quarter-inch so it was square to the blotter and to the keyboard both, and only noticed he had done it after it was done.

---

At ten past four, Priya Ramachandran appeared at the side of his desk with the specific stillness of someone who has been standing there for a while deciding whether to speak.

"Adam. Have you got a minute."

It was not a question and he did not treat it as one. He turned his chair.

She had a printout. That was the first sign that it was bad — nobody under thirty printed anything unless they wanted a physical object to put in front of another human being, which meant she wanted a witness to her own competence, which meant she had already decided she had failed.

"The Brayford valuation," she said.

"Mm."

"I used the wrong discount curve."

"Which one did you use?"

"Last quarter's."

"And it went out."

"It went out Tuesday."

He took the printout and read it. It took him about forty seconds. She stood the whole time, and he was aware of her standing, and he did not hurry, because hurrying would have told her the situation warranted hurry.

"How much does it move the number?" he said.

"One point one per cent."

"Which way?"

"Down. It understates the liability by about eight hundred thousand."

"All right." He set the printout down and squared it to the edge of the desk, and then, conscious of it this time, deliberately nudged it crooked again. "So. Three questions. Has anybody acted on it."

"I don't— I don't think so. The trustees don't meet until the fourteenth."

"That's the first bit of good news, and it's most of the news there is. Second question. Is there anything else in the file that used the same curve."

She hadn't thought about that. He watched her not have thought about it, and watched her decide not to pretend otherwise, and thought better of her for it.

"I'll check."

"Check tonight, not now. Now you're going to be too frightened to see it properly." He said it without emphasis, the way he might have told her the photocopier was on the second floor. "Third question. Who else knows."

"Nobody."

"Then here's what happens." He turned back to his screen and pulled up the Brayford file as he talked, and his voice stayed at the same level it had been at all afternoon. "I'm going to reissue the valuation this evening with a correction note. The note will say the curve was updated following a review of the quarter-end data, which is true, because I'm about to review it. It will not say who ran the first one, because that isn't information the trustees need and it isn't information that improves the document."

"Adam—"

"The second thing that happens is that on Monday you come in and you tell me what you're going to change about how you pull a curve, and it has to be a process, not an intention. 'I'll be more careful' isn't a process. 'I'll diff the curve against the previous run before I build the model' is a process."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Why are you doing this?"

He considered the question, because it deserved considering, and because the honest answer was interesting to him.

"Because the alternative costs the firm eight hundred thousand pounds and costs you your career, and those are wildly disproportionate outcomes for the same mistake. I object to disproportionate outcomes." He glanced up at her. "Also, about one in nine people who make this mistake make it twice. You won't be one of them. I'd put money on it, and I don't gamble."

She laughed — a wet, surprised sound — and then looked furious with herself for it.

"Go home," he said. "Check the file tomorrow."

She went. He reissued the valuation at six forty, with the correction note, and he did in fact review the quarter-end data first, so that the note was true. He had a horror of documents that were not true. It was not a moral position exactly. It was closer to the way some people cannot leave a picture hanging crooked.

He put his coat on at seven. The office was almost empty. On the way past Priya's desk he saw that she had left her printout behind, face-up, and he turned it face-down, and then thought that was probably a strange thing to have done, and left it.

---

The walk from the office to the flat was twenty-two minutes and he had never once taken a different route.

That was not sentimentality. He had timed the three plausible alternatives during his first month at the firm — this was eleven years ago now — and found that one was ninety seconds faster but crossed a junction with a pedestrian phase that failed about one time in six, which meant its variance was worse, and Adam had always preferred a worse mean to a worse variance. He liked to know when he would arrive.

It was March and it had been raining and the pavements had that black shine they get under sodium light. He walked past the shuttered greengrocer, and the launderette with the handwritten sign about the machine that ate coins, and the bus stop with the poster for a film he had no intention of seeing.

At the corner of Lyle Street there was a service station.

He had never once looked at it properly. He had walked past it four thousand times — he had actually done that calculation, at some point, for no reason he could reconstruct — and his eyes slid off it the way they slid off the film poster.

Tonight, for no reason, he stopped.

It was not the same service station. It was not even the same country as the one he was thinking of; the one he was thinking of had been on the A1, four hours north, and had been demolished in 2006. He knew that because he had checked. He had looked it up one night when he was thirty-one and slightly drunk, and found a photograph of the demolition on a local history forum, and had felt precisely nothing about it, and had closed the laptop and gone to bed.

But it had the same lighting. That was all. The same cold blue-white over the pumps, and the same dead square of forecourt, and the same way the light stopped at a hard edge and the dark started.

He had kept the bags neat.

That was the thing that surfaced, when it surfaced, which was rarely and never on purpose. Not the eleven hours. Not the woman behind the counter who had eventually asked if he was all right and whom he had told, politely, that his parents were coming back shortly. Not the police, later, or the aunt, or the eight months after that.

The bags. Two of them, a soft blue holdall and a rucksack with a broken zip, and at some point in the fourth or fifth hour he had noticed that the holdall had slumped over, and he had stood it up again, and squared it against the rucksack, and then he had spent some considerable part of the remaining time making sure they stayed that way.

Because — and this was the part he had never said aloud to anybody, including the three separate therapists his aunt had sent him to — he had believed, with the total conviction of a nine-year-old, that the state of the bags was a variable.

That if they came back and the bags were neat, that would be evidence. Of something. That he had been worth the trip.

Adam stood on the corner of Lyle Street in the rain for about forty seconds, looking at a service station he had never looked at, and then he checked his watch, and saw that he was going to be three minutes late, and walked on.

---

The florist on Pelham Row was shut, which he had known it would be, because it shut at six and it was nearly half past seven. He stood outside it anyway for a moment, the way you do.

There was a bucket left out under the awning with about nine stems in it and a laminated card that said HONESTY BOX — £3 — and an actual box, screwed to the wall, with a slot in the top.

Adam looked at the flowers. They were tulips and they were not in good condition; two of them had gone over entirely and the rest had that translucent look they get on the second day. Three pounds was, objectively, an insult.

He thought about the mathematics of an honesty box on a street with this footfall, and concluded that it almost certainly lost money, and that the florist almost certainly knew that, and kept doing it anyway for reasons that had nothing to do with tulips.

He took six stems. He put a ten-pound note through the slot, because he did not have change and because the idea of standing there doing the sum felt obscene, and then he stood holding six bad tulips in the rain and felt briefly and enormously stupid.

Renne would say they were dying. She would say it the way she said the lamb was dry — as a pre-emptive strike, so that she was the one who had noticed first.

He would say they were fine.

He had bought her flowers on average once every eleven days for eleven years. He knew that number because he had worked it out once, in the same idle way he worked out everything, and had been briefly startled by it: more than three hundred and fifty times. Three hundred and fifty separate occasions of standing somewhere holding stems and feeling stupid.

It had never once occurred to him that this was a large number, or that it meant anything, or that it was in fact the single most consistent thing he had ever done with his life. He had simply done it, the way he took the same route home.

He carried them up Lyle Street with the heads turned down against the rain, which is what you are supposed to do, and which he had learned from a woman in a market in 2009 and had done every time since.

---

She had done the thing with the lamb.

It was genuinely good. Renne was a better cook than she gave herself credit for, and had been since before they married, and the fact that she framed every meal as an experiment that had probably gone wrong was a tic he had stopped trying to correct somewhere around year four.

She took the tulips out of his hand in the doorway before he had his coat off.

"These are dead."

"They're fine."

"Adam, two of these are actually dead."

"Then there are four that aren't, and that's above the long-run average for Pelham Row."

She laughed at that, properly, with her head back, and went and got the green jug from the cupboard over the fridge — not a vase, they had never owned a vase, it had been the green jug for eleven years — and filled it at the tap and put the six stems in it and put the jug in the middle of the table where it would be in the way of everything.

Then she stood looking at it for a second with her hand flat on the table.

He was hanging his coat up and did not see her face.

"It's dry," she said.

"It isn't."

"It's a bit dry."

"Renne. It's not dry."

She smiled at her plate. She had her hair up, and she had put on the grey jumper he liked, and the kitchen smelled of rosemary and the hot-metal smell of the oven cooling, and the window over the sink had gone black and mirrored so that he could see the two of them in it, slightly warped.

They talked about her sister. They talked about the boiler, which was making a sound. She asked about work and he told her about the lapse clustering, because she was one of about three people alive who would let him talk about lapse clustering, and she listened with her chin on her hand and asked a question about halfway through that was better than most of the questions he got at the office.

"So somebody's pushing them," she said.

"Somebody's pushing them or something's happening to them. It's a depot, probably. Closure, restructure, something. The pattern's too tight for coincidence."

"How tight?"

"Four in a fortnight, then eleven weeks of nothing, then three in nine days."

"That could just be—"

"It could. Chance does that. Chance does it about one time in forty, given the exposure." He turned his glass a quarter-turn on the table. "Which is why I wrote 'not urgent' instead of doing anything about it."

"That's very you."

"Is it."

"You said 'one time in forty' like it settles the matter."

"It does settle the matter."

"It settles *a* matter." She was still smiling. "Not necessarily the one you were asked about."

He thought about that. It was a good line and she knew it was a good line, and there was a small silence in which they were both pleased with her.

Then she got up to clear the plates, and he half-rose to help, and she put a hand on his shoulder and pressed him back down into the chair, which she had done perhaps ten thousand times in eleven years, and said, "Sit. You've done your bit," and went to the sink.

He sat. The boiler made its sound. He looked at the black window and at the warped reflection of his wife's back, and he was — he would have said, if anyone had asked him at that exact moment, and he would have meant it — content.

He was thirty-eight years old. He would have said the odds were good.

---

He did not see the knife.

That was the thing he came back to, afterwards, in the small remaining amount of afterwards he had: he did not see it, at any point, and so the fact of it had to be reconstructed rather than remembered. She was at the sink. Then she was not at the sink; she was beside him, and one hand was on his shoulder again, the same hand, the same pressure, and he was in the middle of saying something about the boiler.

And then there was a sensation below his ribs on the right side that his mind, with enormous and embarrassing willingness, categorised as *she has bumped me with her hip*.

He said, "Sorry—" and started to shift the chair out of her way.

The second one was lower and further back, and that one his mind could not categorise as anything, and he made a sound he had never made before.

He got a hand up. Not fast. He was a man who had not been in a fight since he was fourteen and had lost that one. His palm came up flat against her forearm, and he pushed, and the push had almost nothing in it, and she stepped back anyway, the way you step back from a dog that has started barking.

Adam went down the side of the chair and onto the kitchen floor, and the floor was cold through his shirt, and the tiles smelled faintly of the lemon stuff she used.

She was standing about a metre away with the knife held down along her thigh, point at the floor, and she was crying, and she was not moving towards him.

He looked up at her. It was very quiet. The boiler made its sound.

"Renne," he said.

She shook her head. Not a refusal. More like someone trying to get water out of an ear.

He put his hand on his side and it came away wet and black-looking in the kitchen light and he thought, with total clarity: *the light in here is wrong for this, blood isn't that colour, that's just the bulb.*

And then he thought: *eleven months.*

---

It was not a leap. He wanted to be clear about that, later, when there was no later. It was not intuition and it was not a flash of insight, and he would have been embarrassed to have it described as either. It was a calculation, and it took about four seconds, and it went like this.

There was a policy. He had signed it in April of the previous year — not this April, the one before — sitting at this table, with a biro that had run out halfway through so that he had gone to the drawer for another one. Joint life, first death. One point four million.

He had not thought about it since. It was sensible. It was the kind of thing he told other people to do.

April to March was eleven months.

That was the whole of it. Eleven months. And the reason it landed with the weight it landed with, the reason it went through him worse than the knife had, was not that it proved she had planned it.

It was that eleven months is not a plan. Eleven months is a *decision, and then a life*.

Eleven months of getting up. Eleven months of the boiler making its sound and her sister's news and the thing with the lamb. Eleven months of her hand on his shoulder, pressing him back into the chair, *sit, you've done your bit.* Eleven months of being a person who was going to do this, and going to Sainsbury's, and choosing the grey jumper because he liked it.

He had spent his entire professional life explaining to people that the difference between a risk and a certainty is time. That everything unlikely becomes inevitable if you leave it running long enough. He had said it in three different training courses. He had said it to Priya four hours ago, in different words.

He had not once applied it to the woman standing in his kitchen.

"Adam," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "Adam, I'm sorry—"

"Eleven months," he said.

She stopped.

He watched her face do something complicated, and he read it with the same detachment he brought to a claims file, because that was the only instrument he had ever been issued and he was not going to be given another one. She was surprised. Not that he had worked it out. That he had worked it out *now*, at this specific moment, with his hand in his own side.

"You knew I'd—" He had to stop, and breathe, and the breath did not work properly. "You knew I'd do the arithmetic."

She didn't answer.

"That's why you're crying," he said. "Not because of this. Because you knew I'd get there, and you've been dreading it for eleven months, and now it's done."

And Renne Rooke, who was thirty-six, and who had a scar on her knee from a bicycle in 1998, and who could not eat coriander, and whom he had loved without interruption or qualification since a Tuesday in October eleven years ago, said:

"You always get there."

It was, he thought, the most honest thing either of them had said all evening.

---

The cold came up through the tiles into his back and it did not feel like cold. It felt like the floor was taking something from him at a steady rate, and he found he could estimate the rate, and found that the estimate was not encouraging.

She had gone out of the room. He could hear her in the hall. She was on the phone, and she was making the sounds a person makes when they are telling the truth about the wrong thing — *he's, there's, I don't know, please* — and part of him noted, distantly and with something almost like professional respect, that she was doing it well, and that the call would be logged with a timestamp, and that a timestamp was the single most valuable thing she could be acquiring right now.

Nine minutes, maybe. Ambulance to Lyle Street, this hour, this weather. Call it eleven.

He did the sum on the blood and did not like it.

He turned his head. From the floor the kitchen looked entirely different — the underside of the table, the scuffed white of the skirting, a dropped grain of rice under the cupboard that had been there for God knew how long. Things that had been in his house for eleven years and that he had never once seen.

The green jug was directly above him, on the table edge, and from this angle he could see the underside of the tulips. The stems went down into the water and bent where the light bent them, and the two dead ones had their heads right over, and the four that were not dead were still, in fact, not dead.

He had been right about that. It was a very small thing to have been right about.

He found he wanted, quite badly, for someone to come into this kitchen afterwards and take the two dead ones out and leave the four. Not for any reason. He was aware it was not a reasonable thing to want. It sat in him with a weight completely out of proportion to itself, the way the bags had, and he understood — too late, from the floor, in the last clear minutes he was going to get — that it was the same want. It had always been the same want. *Look at how I left it. Look at the state of the thing I was in charge of.*

Nobody took the dead ones out. The flat was cleared by her sister in April and the jug went to a charity shop on the Holloway Road with the flowers still in it, black by then, and the woman who emptied it did not look at what she was tipping into the bin.

He thought about Priya's printout, face-down on her desk.

He thought about the lapse clustering, and about the four hundred thousand pounds, and about the note in the margin that said *Not urgent*, and he thought: *you were wrong about which things were urgent, consistently, for thirty-eight years.*

He thought about the bags.

And this was the part that he would carry — that would, against every physical law he had ever taken on faith, turn out to be something a man could carry — the part that arrived last, when the edges of the room had started to go soft and grey and the sound of her voice in the hall had become a sound rather than words.

He was not angry.

He kept waiting for it. It seemed like the correct thing to feel and he was, if nothing else, a man who liked to produce the correct output. He waited for rage and it did not come, and he waited for grief and got something adjacent to it but wrong, like a word you can't quite place.

What he felt, lying on his own kitchen floor with his own blood going cold around his hip, was *recognition*.

Because of course. Of course this. He had been given a data point at nine years old — a very clean one, unambiguous, eleven hours long and lit blue-white — and he had spent twenty-nine years building an entire life on the assumption that it was an outlier.

It was not an outlier. It was the trend. His parents had left him at a service station. Three men he had known since he was nineteen had taken four years of his work over a single weekend, while he stood at the funeral of a man who had not spoken to him since he was fourteen, and he had gone to that funeral, he had *gone*, because he thought it would have been shabby not to.

And now this. And the sample size was three, which was not enough for anything, which was not *close* to enough for anything, and he knew that, he knew it better than almost anyone alive knew it —

— and he believed it anyway.

That was the last complete thought Adam Rooke had on Earth, and it was not *I hate her*, and it was not *I want to live*.

It was: *I was always disposable, and I have spent my whole life doing the sums for everybody except myself.*

---

The paramedics reached the flat at nine minutes past nine.

They did what they could, which was quite a lot, because two of them were very good and one of them had been doing it for nineteen years. They worked on him on the kitchen floor for six minutes and in the back of the ambulance for eleven more and they did not stop when it was obvious, because they never do.

At the hospital a registrar called it at 9:47 p.m. She wrote the time on a form and did not think about it again for four years, and then thought about it once, in a car park, for no reason at all.

The policy was one point four million pounds. It never paid out. Group life schemes and joint life policies both have the same small ugly clause about unlawful killing, and Renne Rooke had signed it herself, on the same afternoon, with the same borrowed biro, and had not read it, because nobody reads them.

Adam would have found that very funny. He had a good sense of the absurd that almost nobody ever got to see.

He would also have pointed out — kindly, and without making anyone feel stupid — that she had got the assumption wrong, not the arithmetic. People almost always do. The arithmetic is the easy part. It's the assumption underneath it, the one nobody writes down because it seems too obvious to be worth writing, that kills you.

---

There was no light and there was no tunnel and there was no voice.

There was an interval that he could not measure, and being unable to measure it was, briefly, the worst thing that had ever happened to him.

And then there was grass against his cheek.

Wet grass, and a smell of turned earth and something sharper underneath it, like ozone or hot wire. Cold air on the back of his neck. A weight in his limbs that was not the weight of his limbs.

Adam Rooke opened his eyes on a field under two moons, and the first coherent thought he had — before the panic, before the impossible, before any of the rest of it — was that the light was wrong.

Not wrong like the kitchen bulb.

Wrong like the sums were about to be different.

And then, laid over the dark field and the wet grass and the two moons, as clean and as patient as a line of type on a page he had been handed to check:

**ASSESSMENT PENDING.**
**SUBJECT: UNREGISTERED.**
**LOCAL RESIDUE DETECTED — 1 SOURCE, EXPIRING.**

He lay very still in the grass and read it twice.

Then, because he had been an actuary for sixteen years and because a man does not stop being what he is simply because he has stopped being alive, Adam Rooke thought:

*Expiring at what rate?*
