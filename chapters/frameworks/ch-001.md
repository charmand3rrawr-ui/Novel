## Purpose

_Why this chapter exists. Test: what does the novel lose if it is cut?_

This is a **hook** beat inside *Entrance by the Side Door*, which promises: a person with nothing gets one impossible chance. The chapter's job in that promise is to establishing competence in front of people invested in the protagonist not having it.

- **Without it, the novel loses:** its only chance to make a recorded obligation feel like a knife before the reader is invested in anyone. Every later ledger scene draws on credit this one builds
- **It earns its place because:** it is the one chapter where the Tally is a mystery rather than a mechanic. After this the reader knows; here they only feel a wrongness they cannot place

## Goal

- **Plot goal** — what must be true at the end that was not true at the start: Kestrel is through the second door and on the admissions roll, provisionally — and the reader has watched him absorb without being told what they watched
- **Reader goal** — what the reader must feel, suspect, or fear by the last line: they should want him through that door, and be faintly uneasy about the four minutes before dawn that they cannot yet account for
- **Tension calibration** — open around 1, land on 3. Do not exceed the target by more than 1 without logging why.

By the arc's end it will have added a rule and removed an exit — that is how this place answers every problem

## Conflicts

- **External** — the situation, the clock, the opposition. Arc pressure is *institutional gatekeeping*: the admission lapses at noon, the register is missing its middle quire, and the counter-clerk has no incentive to be quick, because slowness is how his counter is governed
- **Interpersonal** — who wants something incompatible with whom: Sila needs one fewer qualified applicant, and doing nothing achieves it
- **Internal** — the protagonist's lie under pressure in this specific way. The lie: *Worth is a balance you must keep positive, and love is the interest paid on it.* he could ask the clerk for the missing quire and simply be handed it. He reconstructs it instead, because a thing given opens an entry he would have to carry
- **What is actually at stake** — the concrete thing that is worse at the end if he loses: if he is not through by noon the pledge that bought his schooling lapses to a house that will sell it rather than hold it

## Shape

**The Demonstration** — Establishing competence in front of people invested in the protagonist not having it.

- **Opening move:** Mid-task, already behind. The reader meets the skill before the biography.
  - _here:_ eleven minutes to noon, Kestrel at the counter reconstructing a standing from a register missing its middle quire. No context and no biography — the skill arrives before the person
- **Turn:** The task turns out to be a test, or the audience turns out to matter.
  - _here:_ the obstruction is the test. The clerk has been timing him, and the second door was never locked
- **Closing move:** Success that costs standing, or success nobody is willing to credit.
  - _here:_ through the door, on the roll, provisionally — and as he turns he understands that being watched was the point, and that he has just made himself worth watching
- **This shape costs:** anonymity
- **Watch for:** The competence is narrated instead of dramatised; the reader is told he is good rather than shown a problem only he could solve.
- **Pairs well with:** witness, the offer — consider for the next chapter.

## Failure modes

_Three specific ways THIS chapter goes wrong. Not generic craft advice._

1. the ledger reconstruction turns into a puzzle the reader is invited to solve. It is a skill they watch, not a riddle they participate in — keep the mechanics legible and the stakes explicit
2. Sila reads as a stock rival. She needs a reason to be in that room that would exist if Kestrel had never been born
3. The competence is narrated instead of dramatised; the reader is told he is good rather than shown a problem only he could solve.
