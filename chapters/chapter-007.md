# Chapter 7 — Per Head

The answer came back in nineteen days, which Wren said was unheard of, and it came back in the form of a man.

His name was Sub-Coadjutor Merrit Oss and he arrived on a Tuesday in a hired car with a driver, and he was thirty-four years old and wore the Accord's grey with the particular crispness of someone who has bought it himself rather than been issued it.

He asked for Ilse Varn. Varn was not there; it was not a Thursday. He was visibly annoyed by this in a way that told Adam, watching from the end of the ward with a mop, that the man had not checked, which told him a number of other things.

He asked for Wren Halloway next, and got her, and spent forty minutes in the intake room with the door shut.

Then the door opened and Wren said, in a voice Adam had not heard her use before:

"Rooke. In here."

---

"You're the orderly," said Oss.

"Yes."

"Sit down."

Adam sat down. He did it slowly and he put both hands on the table where they could be seen, and he had decided on his way across the ward that he was going to be, for the whole of this conversation, precisely as stupid as the man wanted him to be, and not one degree stupider, because overplaying it is how people get caught.

Oss had a folder. He opened it and turned it round.

"Is this your work?"

It was Pell's form. The back of it, the nine observation lines, filled in a cramped hand right to the edge of the box and then continuing, in smaller writing, up the margin.

"I can't read that," said Adam, which was now a lie, and which was the first lie he had told in this building and which he told without any difficulty at all.

"It says," said Oss, "that a *ward orderly* at Mercy House Ninth has identified a systematic defect in the Accord's outcome measure, that the defect arises from inter-house transfer at day four, and that the correct measure is deaths per held-day." He tapped the page twice. "It says this in nine lines and then in four more lines up the side, because Coadjutor Pell ran out of box."

"That sounds like more than nine lines' worth."

"It is considerably more than nine lines' worth." Oss closed the folder. "Did you say those things to him?"

"I said something like them."

"Something like them."

"I don't know the words you've just used. I said that Seventh takes the ones who are getting better, and that means the ones left here are sicker, and that if you count the dead against the beds you'll get a worse number every year even if nobody does anything wrong." Adam kept his hands on the table. "And I said you should count the days instead, because the house already writes the days down."

There was a silence.

Sub-Coadjutor Merrit Oss looked at him for rather a long time.

"Where did you train?"

"I didn't."

"Everybody says that."

"I imagine they do," said Adam.

Oss took a second folder out of the case. This one was thinner.

"Adam Rooke. Admitted to Mercy House Ninth on the ninth of Thaw, unaccompanied, no papers, no house of origin, no parish. Intake officer recorded 'presented as lost, no injury, no illness, states he cannot read.'" He looked up. "Halloway wrote 'no story' in the margin. Do you know what that means on an intake sheet?"

"No."

"It means she didn't believe you and wrote it down anyway so that nobody could say later she'd hidden it." Oss turned a page. "Which is, incidentally, why I'm inclined to take this house seriously. Nobody falsifies a record they've already annotated against themselves."

He closed the folder.

"So. No papers. No parish. No trade. Illiterate. And nineteen days ago you took apart a measurement system that four hundred people in the grey have used without complaint for a generation." He folded his hands. "Do you see my difficulty?"

"You think I'm somebody."

"I think you are *obviously* somebody," said Merrit Oss. "The question is which kind. There are four, in my experience, and I'd like to work out which one before I leave this room, so I'm going to tell you what they are and watch your face, and I'd advise you not to bother arranging it, because I'm quite good at this and you're not a professional."

Adam said nothing. He let his hands stay exactly where they were.

"One. You're an educated man who has lost his memory, in which case you are a tragedy and no threat to anyone." Oss held up a finger. "Two. You're an educated man running from a debt, a marriage or a magistrate, in which case you are somebody else's problem and I'd wish you well."

"Three?"

"Three, you're an educated man who has been *placed* here." He said it without any change of tone. "By Second, or by Seventh, or by somebody with an interest in what the Accord measures. In which case the document I have in my folder is not an insight. It's an opening move, and Ninth is the piece being sacrificed."

There was a long pause.

"And four?" said Adam.

"Four," said Oss, "is that you are exactly what you appear to be, which is a man with no history and an extraordinary mind, and that both of those things are true at once, and that nobody anywhere can explain it." He watched Adam's face. "Four is the one I like least. Three I can investigate. Four I have to *decide* about."

Adam let the silence run for four seconds, because a man who answers immediately has prepared.

"I can't help you with any of that," he said. "I don't know which one I am either."

"That is either the most honest thing anyone has said to me this month," said Oss, "or the single best answer available, and I cannot tell which, and that is precisely my difficulty."

He stood up and went to the window and looked out at the yard.

"Let me tell you why I came in person," he said, "because it isn't the measure. The measure is good and it will go to a board and it will probably pass and I did not need to see your face for that."

"Then why?"

"Because of the fourteen lines up the margin." Oss did not turn round. "Pell wrote nine lines of your argument, and then he ran out of box, and then he wrote fourteen more lines up the side of a form he has filled in three hundred times in eleven years. Do you understand what that is?"

"Enthusiasm."

"It's *conversion*," said Merrit Oss. "I have known Coadjutor Pell for six years. He is a decent, unimaginative, permanently exhausted man who has never once exceeded a box. And a ward orderly talked to him for nineteen minutes and he came back and wrote up the side of a form like a student."

He turned round.

"I did not come here to check your arithmetic," he said. "I came to see what you did to him."

---

Oss stayed four hours, and in the fourth hour he made a mistake, and Adam spent the following two weeks trying to work out whether it had been one.

He had asked for the ledgers. Wren had given them to him. He had gone through four years at a speed that told Adam he was genuinely competent — he found the day-count column himself in under a minute, which had taken Adam four hours — and at the end of it he sat back and said, to nobody in particular:

"It's worse at Fourth."

Wren's head came up. "Fourth?"

"Mercy House Fourth. Riverside." Oss was still looking at the page. "Same pattern. Steeper. They transfer to Second." He seemed to remember where he was. "That's not for this room."

"You've checked Fourth already," said Adam.

"I checked six houses on the train."

*Nineteen days,* thought Adam. *The form went in nineteen days ago. He has read it, checked six houses, and come here in person, and he is a sub-coadjutor and this is not his district.*

"Can I ask a stupid question," he said.

"You've asked four."

"If the measure's wrong, who's it been wrong in favour of?"

Merrit Oss stopped.

It was a small thing. He had been closing the ledger and he did not stop moving exactly, but there was a hitch in it, half a beat, the sort of thing Adam had watched for across a table for sixteen years.

"That's not a stupid question," said Oss.

"I thought it might not be."

"It's the only question." The young man looked up, and for a second the crispness went out of him entirely and what was underneath was tired and about nine years older. "And I would advise you — genuinely, and I'm not going to say it again — not to ask it of anybody else in the grey."

---

He left at six. He shook Wren's hand, which surprised her, and he did not shake Adam's.

At the gate he turned round.

"Rooke."

"Sir."

"Deaths per held-day is going to a review board in the spring." He put his hat on. "If it passes, eleven houses get more money and four get less, and the four are going to be very unhappy, and somebody is going to want to know who started it."

"And you'll tell them Pell."

"I'll tell them Pell, because Pell wrote it, and Pell is fifty-two and has a pension and does not care." Oss opened the car door. "But Pell will tell them you. Not out of malice. Because he's an honest man and he thinks you should get the credit, and honest men are how everybody I have ever protected has been found."

The car went down the road and Adam stood at the gate for a while.

*Flagged,* he thought. *Observed.*

*And now named.*

---

He found it eleven days later, and he found it because of a boiled sweet.

Petren had been paid, as always, per page. And Petren, being nine, had been spending the sweets at the stall on the corner of the Quarry Road, and had come back one evening with a story about the man at the stall being angry about the price of something, and in the middle of the story he had used a word Adam had not heard before.

*Capitation.*

"What's that word?"

"It's what Dala's brother gets," said Petren, with his mouth full. "He works at Seventh. He says they get it for every one they take in."

Adam sat down on the bench very slowly.

"Say that again."

"He gets *capitation*," said Petren, pleased to have an audience. "Dala says it's why he's always in a good mood at the end of the month."

---

It took him four days to confirm it and he confirmed it without ever once asking a direct question, because by the fourth week on Vaurun he had learned that a direct question is a thing people remember.

He did it over four evenings, in the laundry, because Dala did the sheets in the evenings and liked company and talked continuously and remembered almost nothing of what she had said.

He had worked out the method on the first night and had felt unclean about it for approximately ninety seconds.

*Never ask. Offer a wrong fact and let her correct you.*

"Your brother's at Seventh," he said, on the first evening, folding badly on purpose so she would take it off him.

"Eleven years." She took the sheet off him. "You fold like a man."

"I fold like a man who's carried water for a month. Is it a good place?"

"It's a *big* place." She said it the way people say things they have decided about long ago. "Two surgeons. Fan that works. They've a panel in the hall that's lit and it tells you who's on."

"Sounds like he did well to get in."

"He did well to *stay*," said Dala. "They turn people over there. Not like here. Wren's had the same four of us nine years."

Adam filed that and did not touch it.

On the second evening he said: "I suppose he's paid the same as you, roughly."

Dala laughed for nine seconds.

He waited it out, folding.

"Bless you," she said. "Ninth pays a wage. Seventh pays a wage *and*."

"And overtime."

"And a bit for every one through the door."

He kept folding. He did not look up. He had learned, in sixteen years across a table, that the two seconds after a person says the load-bearing thing are the two seconds in which they decide whether they have said too much, and that the single most effective instrument available is a man doing something else with his hands.

"That'd be for the night intake, I suppose," he said. "The ambulance ones."

"For *all* of them." Dala snapped a sheet out. "Every admission. Don't matter where from. He says the transfer days are the good ones because they come in walking and you've only the paper to do."

Adam Rooke folded a sheet in a laundry on a world with two moons and felt the whole thing go click.

"That seems a strange way to pay a hospital," he said mildly.

"It's how they do it there."

"Mm," said Adam, and asked her about the boiler.

Dala talked about the boiler for eleven minutes. She had strong views about the boiler. She never thought about the conversation again, and eleven months later, when it mattered a great deal and three people asked her whether she had ever discussed Seventh's payments with anybody, she answered honestly that she had not.

---

He asked Dala about her brother, who was well, and whose wife was expecting, and who had been at Seventh eleven years.

He asked her what Seventh was like, and was told it was big, and modern, and had two surgeons and a working extraction fan and a data panel that was lit.

He asked whether her brother had ever thought about coming to Ninth, where it was closer, and Dala laughed for about nine seconds and said that Ninth paid a wage and Seventh paid a wage *and*, and when he asked *and what*, she said: "And a bit for every one through the door. It's how they do it there. It's why they take everyone."

He said, "That seems sensible," and changed the subject to the boiler, and Dala talked about the boiler for eleven minutes and never once thought about the conversation again.

Then he sat in the store room that night and put it together, and what he put together was not a conspiracy, and that was the part that took him longest to accept.

*Seventh is paid per admission. Not per bed. Not per outcome. Per head through the door.*

*So Seventh wants heads. Every head is money. And the cheapest head to acquire is a patient who is already stable, already diagnosed, already four days into a recovery that somebody else paid for — because that head costs Seventh almost nothing and counts exactly the same as a head that arrives bleeding at two in the morning.*

*Ninth is paid by allocation against outcomes. Seventh is paid by volume.*

*And the two schemes were designed by different people, at different times, for different good reasons, and nobody has ever put them on the same page.*

He sat with his back against the shelving.

*Nobody is stealing. Nobody has done anything wrong. There is no villain. There is a house that is rewarded for taking and a house that is punished for being taken from, and the interface between them is a transfer form that a tired registrar signs on the fourth day.*

*It has been running for nine years.*

*It will run forever, because there is no single person anywhere in the system who can see both halves.*

He sat with that for a long time, and then made himself do the last part, which was the part he had been circling since the laundry.

*And I only found it because a nine-year-old with a hole in his heart spent his wages on sweets at the right stall.*

That was the whole of it. Not method. Not insight. He had built an elegant search and it had produced nothing for four weeks, and the thing that actually cracked it had walked in with its mouth full and told him for free.

He turned it over and did not like the shape of it.

*So the model is wrong. Not the conclusion — the conclusion holds. The* model *of how I find things.*

He had been proceeding as though information on this world were like information in a claims file: static, complete, sitting in a document somewhere, waiting for somebody with the wit to measure the columns. It was not. Nothing important here was written down. The capitation was not in a ledger, it was in a man's good mood at the end of the month, observed by his sister, mentioned to a child.

*Which means the ledgers are the least valuable thing in this building,* he thought, *and I have spent a month on them because they were the part that looked like home.*

He lay back on the mattress.

*And it means the most valuable asset I have is not the arithmetic.*

*It's that four people in this house talk to me while their hands are busy.*

He did not enjoy the sentence, and he did not stop it, and he was aware — distantly, the way you are aware of weather coming — that he had just described his own kindness as a holding.

---

He told Varn on the Thursday and she did not believe him until he showed her the shape of it, and then she went outside into the yard and did not come back for eleven minutes.

When she came back she was steady.

"How much?" she said.

"Per head? Dala thinks about a third of a crown. I'd guess higher, because people always understate what other people earn." Adam had the figures on the bench in chalk. "Ninth transfers between nine and fourteen a month. Call it eleven. That's three and a half to four crowns a month going to Seventh for patients Ninth admitted, stabilised and kept."

"Which is more than our entire endowment."

"It's about sixty per cent of your endowment, and it is roughly the difference between running at a loss and not."

Varn put both hands on the bench.

"I have sent them eleven hundred people," she said.

"Yes."

"I have sent them eleven hundred people, on the fourth day, every time, because Seventh has the beds and we do not, and every single time I have thought I was doing the right thing for the patient."

"You were," said Adam. "That's what makes it work. If it hurt them you'd have stopped."

She laughed. It was not a good sound.

"Can you prove it?"

"No."

"Then what use—"

"I can prove the *pattern*," said Adam. "I can prove day-four transfers, I can prove the distribution has shifted left nine years running, and I can prove your outcome measure is geography. That's already gone in and it's going to a board in the spring." He wiped his hands on his shirt. "What I can't prove is the capitation, because I have a nine-year-old's word and a conversation about a boiler."

"Then get the proof."

"No," said Adam.

Varn looked up sharply.

"I'll tell you exactly why," he said, "and then you can tell me I'm a coward, and I'll agree with you, and we'll both be right." He turned to face her. "If I go looking for Seventh's payment records, I am an unregistered orderly with no name investigating a chartered house. Within a fortnight somebody asks who I am. Within a month somebody finds out that nobody knows, because I have no papers, no history, and no story that survives four questions."

"Wren would vouch—"

"Wren would vouch for me and they would ask her what she actually knows, and she is a scrupulously honest woman, and the answer is *nothing*." He held her eye. "And the moment they have that, this stops being about Seventh and starts being about me, and the board in the spring never hears the held-day argument at all, because it arrived attached to a man who turned out to be a problem."

The theatre was quiet.

"So we let them have it," said Varn.

"We let them have it, for now, and we win the measure in the spring, because the measure is the thing that actually changes the money." Adam picked up the chalk and put it down again. "And then, when the measure has passed and nobody can unpick it, somebody with a name and a licence and a pension goes and asks Seventh a question about capitation."

"Who?"

"Not me," said Adam Rooke. "I'm going to be somewhere else by then, and I'm going to have papers."

---

He told Wren last, and he told her badly, and it was the only thing in the whole four weeks he would have done differently.

He told her in the intake room at eleven at night with the ledger box between them, and he told her the way he had told Varn — mechanism first, then consequence, then the number — because it was the order that worked and he had used it twice that fortnight and it had worked both times.

He was about four sentences in when he realised she had stopped moving.

"—so the transfers aren't medical," he said. "Or rather they are, but that isn't why the system keeps producing them, and—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

Wren Halloway sat with her hands flat on the table, looking at the wall, and did not say anything for about eleven seconds.

"Aden Farrow," she said.

"I don't know that name."

"No. You wouldn't." She was still looking at the wall. "He came in on a Saturday four years ago with a crushed foot and he was here nine days and he was doing well, and on the fourth day I had the transfer paper and I didn't sign it, because he had nobody and Seventh's a long way and he'd have been on his own."

"Wren—"

"So I kept him. And he got the rot in it on the ninth day, which happens, and it happens here more than at Seventh because we've a fan that doesn't work and they've one that does." Her voice had not changed at all. "And he died, and I wrote him in the book, and every year since, when the figures go up, I have thought about Aden Farrow and I have thought: *that one is yours, Halloway. You kept him because you were sentimental and it cost him.*"

The lamp hissed.

"Four years," said Wren. "Four years I've carried that. And you're telling me the paper I didn't sign was never about him at all."

Adam sat very still.

He had, he understood with total clarity, walked into this room with a mechanism and a consequence and a number, and had not once considered that the ledger on the table between them was not data.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I came in here and did it like a document."

"You did."

"I'd like to do it again properly and I don't know how."

Wren turned her head and looked at him then, and whatever was in her face, it was not anger.

"No," she said. "You don't. That's the thing about you and I've been trying to name it for a month." She pushed the ledger box an inch away from herself. "You're not cold. I've met cold. Cold doesn't stay up all night with a woman in cot nine and it doesn't pay a child a rate so he'll not run out of goodwill."

"Then what?"

"You're *sighted*," said Wren Halloway. "You see the shape of a thing so fast that you're already three streets ahead before anybody else has got their coat on, and by the time you look back at us you've forgotten that we're still standing in the hall."

She got up and went to the door, and stopped there with her hand on the frame.

"Aden Farrow," she said. "Say it back to me."

"Aden Farrow."

"Right." She did not turn round. "Next time you bring me a mechanism, bring me the name first. There's always a name. You'll find it makes the mechanism slower to explain and you'll find I understand it a great deal faster."

---

He was awake most of that night and it was not the Accord that kept him awake.

It was that he had enjoyed it.

He lay on the mattress in the store room with his hands behind his head and made himself look at it directly, the way he had made himself look at the repricing on the road and the yield decay in the field, because a man who does not finish the sentence is simply a man who intends to think it again later with less supervision.

Four weeks ago he had been an illiterate nobody in a field with three units off a dying beast.

Tonight he had a supplier under a six-year agreement, a measure going to a review board, an endowment argument in reserve, and a complete map of a nine-year financial pathology that nobody else on the planet could see.

And he had assembled all of it out of a nine-year-old's sweets, a column width, and four conversations in which he had never once asked a direct question.

*I am extremely good at this,* he thought. *I was good at it there and I am better at it here, because here nobody is looking.*

It was true and it was not the frightening part.

The frightening part was the thing underneath it, which he got to at about three in the morning and which arrived with the cold, complete clarity that had been the great curse of both his lives.

*Every single thing I have done in this building has been in service of keeping it open.*

*And every single thing I have done in this building has also been practice.*

He turned over.

*The pump was practice. The ratio was practice. Kassick was practice — that was a man being manoeuvred into a position where his least painful option was the one I wanted, and I did it in four minutes in a yard, and he thanked me.*

*I am learning how this world's institutions come apart. I am learning it on a charity hospital, on people I like, for their own good, and I am getting better at it very fast.*

He looked at the dark ceiling.

*And on the day I want something that is not their good, I will already know how.*

He did not say *not yet*.

He noticed that he did not say it, and he lay in the dark and waited to see whether he would, and he did not, and eventually the small white moon went past the high window and he slept.

---

In the morning there was a card at the gate.

It was slid under the bar where the latch went, folded once, with no name on the outside, and Wren found it and brought it to him in the yard because it had four words on the inside and one of them was his.

*Rooke. The Quarry Road stall. Sixth hour. — O.*

He looked at it for a while.

"Who's O?" said Wren.

"A man who was here nineteen days after a form went in," said Adam, "and who told me not to ask a question, which is not the same as telling me the answer is no."

He folded the card and put it in his shirt.

"Are you going?"

Adam Rooke looked up the road towards the Quarry Road, where a stall sold boiled sweets to a nine-year-old with a hole in his heart, and where a sub-coadjutor of the Ninefold Accord wanted to meet an orderly who could not read.

"Yes," he said. "Obviously."

"It could be a trap."

"It almost certainly is one. That's not a reason." He turned the card over in his fingers. "It's an unsigned card at a public stall in daylight. If he wanted me taken he'd have taken me on Tuesday in your intake room with a driver in the yard, and nobody would have asked him a single question, because I am a man with no papers and no parish and Ninth would have been told I'd been assisted."

Wren folded her arms against the cold.

"Then what is it?"

"It's a man who told me not to ask a question," said Adam, "in a room where he knew he was being recorded by his own memory, and who has now arranged somewhere he isn't." He put the card in his shirt. "He's not investigating me any more. He's decided which of the four I am."

"Which one?"

"I don't know. That's the interesting part." He picked up the empty buckets, out of habit, and then remembered the pump and set them down again, and the small absurdity of it stayed with him all the way up the road. "If he's decided I'm the third one — placed here by somebody — then he wants to know who, and he'll offer me something to find out."

"And if it's the fourth?"

Adam stopped at the gate.

The fourth. *A man with no history and an extraordinary mind, and both of those things true at once, and nobody anywhere can explain it.*

"Then he wants to use me," he said, "and he'll be honest about it, and it will be the first honest offer I have had on this world."

He went out under the lintel he could now read, and did not look up at it, which he noticed, and which he thought about for most of the walk.
