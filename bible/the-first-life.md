# The First Life

Earth. Thirty-eight years, ending on a kitchen floor. It occupies chapters 1–9
directly and the whole novel indirectly.

**Rule:** the first life is never explained in summary. It surfaces in fragments,
triggered by the present, and the reader assembles it (`LK-EARNED-WOUND`).

## The three betrayals

### The abandonment (age 9)

A service station four hours from home. He was told to wait with the bags. He
waited eleven hours, and the detail he keeps is not the fear — it is that he
kept the bags neat, because he thought the state of the bags might matter to
whether they came back.

*What it installed:* if he is good enough, useful enough, tidy enough, he will
be kept. The lie that runs him.

### The theft (age 33)

Four years building a company with three people he had known since he was
nineteen. They moved the assets over a weekend while he was at his father's
funeral — a funeral for a man who had not spoken to him in twenty-four years,
which he attended anyway, which is the part that matters.

*What it installed:* the people who take from you are the people who know your
schedule.

### The knife (age 38)

His wife, Renne, took out the policy eleven months before she used it. The
figure was £1.4 million. He worked out the eleven months while he was bleeding,
because working things out is what he does instead of feeling them, and the
novel should let the reader notice that before he does.

*What it installed:* everything. Every attachment is a policy someone has taken
out on you.

## What he brought

Nothing physical. What survived:

- **The arithmetic.** He was an actuary. He assessed risk professionally, and
  he cannot stop. On Vaurun this reads as uncanny insight; it is only grief
  with a method.
- **The names.** Nine of them. He has never written them down.
- **The eleven months.** The specific interval is the thing he returns to. It
  proves it was not a moment of rage. It was *planned*, and planning is the one
  thing he respects.
- **The bags.** He still keeps his belongings unnaturally neat.

## What Earth is for

Three functions, and nothing else:

1. To make the system's logic emotionally inevitable — of course a man taught
   that attachment is a policy would be handed a power that converts the living
   into material.
2. To give the reader one memory of him *before* — the novel needs a version of
   this man who was kind, and it needs the reader to have seen it.
3. To provide the ending's measure. In the last movement he is asked what he
   wanted. The honest answer is on Earth, in chapter 4, and it is small.

## Renne

She is not a monster and the book must not let her be one. She is a woman who
made a plan over eleven months. The reader should be able to construct her
reasoning without being given it, and should find that it very nearly holds.

He never sees her again. That is the point — the person he is avenging himself
on is not available, so the debt gets charged to everyone else.
