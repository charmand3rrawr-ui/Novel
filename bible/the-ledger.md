# The Ledger

The world's central system, and the thing the protagonist is good at.

## What it is

A ledger is a legally binding record of obligation kept by a chartered house.
Every entry names an obligor, an obligee, a consideration, and a term. Entries
are enforceable by any chartered house, not only the one holding them
(`LK-LEDGER-BINDING`), which is why a debt taken in a river town can be
collected in a station corridor eleven years later.

## The four instruments

1. **Note** — a simple obligation. Money, labour, or goods, with a term.
2. **Standing** — a continuing obligation with no fixed term. Rent, tithe,
   service. The instrument that quietly runs most people's lives.
3. **Pledge** — an obligation secured against a thing. Property, tools, a
   licence, and in rare and legal cases a child (`LK-COLLATERAL`).
4. **Discharge** — the record of an obligation ending. The rarest document in
   the world, and the most forged.

## Why it cannot be cheated

Entries cannot be deleted, only superseded (`LK-NO-ERASURE`). A correction is
itself an entry, and reads as one. This makes the books honest and the houses
dishonest: the way to hide something is never to erase it but to bury it under
a true entry that points somewhere else. The protagonist's craft is exactly
this — reading the shape of what a true record was designed to make you
overlook.

## What it cannot process

A gift. There is no instrument for a transfer with no consideration and no
term. Houses treat unrecorded generosity as either fraud or evidence of a
private arrangement, and have been wrong about it for four hundred years.

This is the spine of the novel: the protagonist is the finest reader of the
books in their generation, and the story ends with them doing the one thing the
books have no column for.

## Craft ladder (maps to the `craft` progression track)

| Value | Competence |
|---|---|
| 1 | Reads a clean note and finds the term |
| 2 | Reconstructs a standing from partial entries — the academy threshold |
| 3 | Spots a burying entry |
| 4 | Traces an obligation across two houses |
| 5 | Builds a burying entry that survives review |
| 6 | Values a person the way the house does, and knows they are doing it |
| 7 | Reads a house's intent from its record-keeping habits |
| 8 | Can construct a discharge that is legitimate and ruinous |
| 9 | Can make the books say what happened |
| 10 | Can make them say what should have |
